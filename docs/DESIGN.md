# Edge of the Unknown — Design Document
## An Exploration-First Space Game of Trade, Discovery, and Alien Diplomacy

*Version 0.2 — June 2026 (exploration-first revision)*

---

## 1. Purpose and Scope

Edge of the Unknown is a game of space exploration and discovery, presented as a modern terminal application using Python 3.12+ and the Textual TUI framework. The player's goal is to push outward from the Core Space — the protected central region governed at the outset by the Federation — into an unknown warp-connected universe and find what is out there: uncharted planets that can be descended onto, derelict shipwrecks, nebulae and black holes, strange space-borne entities, and the ruins, artifacts, and ancient technology of lost civilizations. A port pair-trading loop provides the economy — but as a means to an end. Trading funds the faster engines, stronger shields, better sensors, cloaking devices, and armaments needed to travel farther, survive hostile space, and reach rarer and more valuable discoveries. The galaxy is inhabited by alien species whose disposition runs a continuous scale from openly hostile to warmly friendly: most lean friendly and offer technology for barter or for the universal currency, gold-pressed latinum; the hostile-leaning aliens are the escalating price of deep space, with the deadliest among them also the rarest. Species are bound into rival **alliances** the player can join — but only one at a time, so winning one bloc's favor forfeits its rivals' — over a field of unaligned wild cards, and they hold stances toward *each other* as well as toward the player. The Federation is simply the alliance that governs the Core Space at the start; control of that home region can pass to another alliance through the player's deeds or the galaxy's own upheavals, and with it, whether the Core remains a safe harbor.

The remainder of this document specifies our design. The lineage and influences that shaped it are summarized in the Inspiration section below.

### 1.1 Inspiration

**TradeWars 2002.** Edge of the Unknown is built on the mechanical bones of TradeWars 2002 (TW2002), the classic BBS door game. The warp-graph universe, port economy, turn system, and engine foundations are inherited from TW2002 — most visibly in the retained port pair-trading loop — and the exploration-first design builds on them, free to diverge as its own concepts take shape rather than holding TW2002 fidelity as a constraint. This foundation is informed by direct source analysis of seven existing TradeWars clones and the original 1986 TradeWars II BASIC source; Appendix A summarizes what each codebase taught us.

**Lightspeed (MicroProse, 1990).** The exploration-first reframing draws on Lightspeed, in which a lone scout pushes into an unknown cluster where habitable worlds are rare and aliens must be befriended or eliminated to secure the region. From it we take the engine-room subsystem/component ship model (§4.1: slotted subsystems built from a shared component vocabulary, localized combat damage, hybrid repair), the planetary type taxonomy with yield profiles and habitability caps (§4.2), and the per-species alien dialogue corpus that informs our dialogue system (§6.7). Appendix B details what we took from Lightspeed and what we left behind.

## 2. Design Goals and Non-Goals

**Goals.** An exploration-first core loop — venture out, discover, return, upgrade, venture farther — layered on a TW2002-derived foundation (warp graph, turns per day, port pair-trading, single-keystroke commands, ANSI-flavored aesthetics); a tangible risk/reward gradient in which discovery rarity, technology value, and danger all scale with distance from the Core Space; trading as the reliable income floor and discovery as the progression ceiling; deterministic, seedable universe and rules engine with full unit-test coverage of game math; single-player first with a clean path to LAN/hosted multiplayer; modern TUI affordances layered on top (clickable warps, sortable tables, built-in route planner, discovery codex); everything configurable (universe size, economy constants, ship stats, alien species rosters, discovery tables) via versioned config files.

**Non-goals (v1).** Telnet/BBS door compatibility; TWGS protocol compatibility with existing TW2002 helper tools; massive concurrency (we design for tens of players, not hundreds); pixel graphics; full 4X-style diplomacy or empire simulation (an alien species' attitude is a simple score, not a diplomacy tree).

**Command/event flow.** The TUI (or a bot, or a network client) submits a `Command` to `server.service`. The service validates it against a `SessionContext` (terminal-space pattern), calls the appropriate pure reducer in `core.rules`, which returns `(new_state_delta, [events])`. Events are appended to an event log table (twclone's durable rail) and fanned out to subscribed sessions; the engine's tick loop consumes the same log for background reactions. All randomness flows through a seeded `random.Random` owned by the game state, so any game is fully reproducible from `(seed, command log)` — which is also our save-game integrity check and our regression-test harness.

**Players join via a recorded command, not the big bang.** Universe generation (§5) seeds only the shared, seed-derived world — ports, planets, aliens, discoveries, the Stardock, the governing alliance — and **no players**. A player enters through a `JoinGame` command reduced by `core.rules` like any other: it allocates the player and a starter hull at the configured start sector, seeds starting balances/turns and membership of the Core's governing alliance, and opens the Stardock as a pre-explored route. Because joining is an ordinary log entry, `new_game` enrolls player 1 by appending `JoinGame` (so it lands in the durable command log), `rebuild` replays it to reconstruct the player deterministically, and additional players join the *same* universe by appending their own `JoinGame` — the seam multiplayer (§14, Phase 4) builds on. Keeping player creation out of the seed-derived world is what makes "reproducible from `(seed, command log)`" hold for players too. **Identity stays out of core (Phase 4, WP64, H15):** accounts, salted PBKDF2 password hashes, and session tokens live in a **separate server-side SQLite** (`edge/server/accounts.py`), never in `UniverseState` or `state_hash`. The lobby (`edge/server/net.py::LobbyServer`) authenticates a connection, then `join_game` claims a seat — a pre-enrolled but unbound `player_id` if one is free (a fresh game's auto-enrolled player 1 becomes the first human's seat), else a freshly appended `JoinGame` — and records the account↔game↔player binding; `resume` re-binds an account to its existing seat. Because the seat only ever enters the game as a logged `JoinGame`, the roster still rebuilds from the command log alone. One authoritative single-writer task hosts each open game (§14, H14).

**Fog of war at the boundary.** Internal models are complete; everything sent to a client passes through `to_public(context)` projections that strip unexplored warps, unseen port stock, and cloaked ships, exactly as terminal-space does.

**Single-player = embedded server.** The Textual app instantiates `service.GameService` in-process with a background asyncio task running `engine.ticker`. Multiplayer later swaps the in-process call for JSON-RPC over websockets without changing the TUI's interface to the service.

Commodities are the canonical TW2002 trio — Fuel Ore, Organics, Equipment — with port classes the eight buy/sell triples (terminal-space enum, §A.2) plus Class 0/9 Stardock selling hardware. BNT's fourth commodity (energy) is deliberately omitted to keep the canonical trio.

*(Phase 5 adds two top-level hashed state containers alongside the entity maps: `port_orders` — each port's open buy/sell orders under the order book (§8) — and `notices` — the Stardock tavern noticeboard, a capped ring of player-posted messages (§14). Both are reconstructed by replay like every other hashed state.)*

**Currency.** The universal currency is **gold-pressed latinum** ("latinum"), measured in **slips** — the standard unit of account, so balances and prices read as e.g. `14,250 slips`. All port prices, bank balances, hardware costs, and alien technology offers are denominated in it; high-value alien tech may additionally (or exclusively) demand barter in artifacts and recovered technology from the player's discoveries.

**Ship aspects.** Every ship is described by a common set of aspects: *cargo capacity* (holds), *shields* (max + regen), *engine speed*, *cloak/stealth rating* (chance to avoid detection by hostile aliens; 0 = none), *sensor rating* (chance to detect hidden discoveries and to spot hostiles first), and *armaments* (a spinal Main Gun plus homing missiles; fighters too at Phase 3). Following Lightspeed, "engine speed" splits into two distinct aspects: **warp speed** (the Spindrive — governs `turns_per_warp` for strategic travel) and **combat speed** (the Thrusters — the in-fight evasion/flee input). Alien technology upgrades improve individual aspects within hull-defined caps. For the player ship (and any hull authored with a `subsystems` block, §4.1) the shield / warp-speed / combat-speed / gun aspects are **derived from installed components** rather than stored as flat scalars — but everything downstream (flee math, hit chance, turns-per-warp) still reads them as plain aspect values. Ship types are config data, not code (ExchangeConflict's ships.json generalized): each type defines per-aspect base values and caps (or a subsystem slot layout), turns-per-warp, offensive/defensive odds multipliers, and price. v1 ships at minimum: Merchant Cruiser / Trailblazer-class (starter, 20–75 holds per the original data), Scout Marauder, Missile Frigate, BattleShip, Imperial StarShip, plus alien NPC hulls.

**Ship and starbase classes (config schema).** Generalizing ExchangeConflict's `ships.json` against the Lightspeed stat-block model, every hull — player, NPC, and starbase — is one `ship_class` config record:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `id`, `name` | e.g. `broodmaster_battleship`, "Battleship" |
| `role` | `fighter` / `large_fighter` / `warship` / `capital_warship` / `transport` / `light_craft` / `starbase` |
| `owner_species` | which species fields it (null for player/neutral hulls) |
| `length_m` | hull length — the Ship Size Comparison axis; feeds a size term in detection and hit-chance. Larger hulls also afford more subsystem slots (upgrade headroom) at the cost of a bigger detection/hit profile |
| `speed` | base engine rating — splits into warp speed (`turns_per_warp`, strategic) and combat speed (the flee term, §10); on a hull with a `subsystems` block these derive from the Spindrive and Thrusters instead |
| `aspects` | per-aspect base values + caps (holds, shields, cloak, sensors) — used for hulls *without* a `subsystems` block (most NPC hulls); otherwise the slotted aspects are derived (§4.1) |
| `subsystems` | *optional* (§4.1) — per-subsystem slot layout `{slot_id, legal_components, base_component\|empty}` enabling localized component damage, field repair, and component upgrades. Present on the player starter and any hull we want to support these on |
| `armament` | list of `weapon` ids (Main Gun = a `spinal` weapon; missiles = a `homing` consumable, §4.1) |
| `defenses` | list of `defense` ids |
| `turns_per_warp`, `odds_mult` (off/def), `price` | as for player ship types |
| `immobile` | true for starbases |

A **`weapon`** record is `{name, damage, firing_arc, rate, special}` where `firing_arc ∈ {ahead, all_round, spinal}` (spinal = fires only dead-ahead and only periodically — the counter is to strafe past its firing line), `rate ∈ {continuous, periodic}`, and `special` names a behavior hook (`homing`, `neutron_spark`, `blaster`, `engine_flux`, `proton_stalker_pod`, …; see §6 signature mechanics). A **`defense`** record is `{type, value}` with `type ∈ {laser_turret, armour, screens, energy_plates, speed_and_size}` — `energy_plates` and `screens` are damage-reducing layers, `speed_and_size` is the implicit defense of small fast hulls. A **starbase** is simply a `ship_class` with `role=starbase` and `immobile=true`, given shields/hull, armament, and a `destructible` flag; per-species `starbase_policy` (§6) decides whether and where one is placed. Because alliance admission prices are paid in razed starbases (§6), starbases are first-class destructible targets, not scenery.

### 4.1 Engine Room: Subsystems and Components

Borrowing the engine-room model of the BBS-era game *Lightspeed* (and discarding its cockpit, targeting, and fighter-launch layers, which we do not model), a player ship's aspect scalars are **not stored directly but derived from a small set of slotted subsystems**, each assembled from generic, interchangeable **components**. One mechanism then serves three jobs at once: combat damage becomes **localized** (a hit knocks out a component, degrading exactly the matching aspect), repair becomes **tangible** (swap a part), and upgrades become **physical** (install a better-tier part, or fill an empty slot). This is the concrete, in-world face of the "alien technology upgrades aspects within hull-defined caps" rule above. It is deliberately kept **bounded so it never becomes a parts-management chore**: only a handful of subsystems, each with 2–4 slots, drawn from one shared parts vocabulary, with no adjacency or layout puzzles.

**The four slotted subsystems.** Every aspect that combat can degrade lives in one of these (sensors and cloak remain single-module aspects — a sensor suite, a cloak module — to keep the engine room focused and the burden low):

| Subsystem | Derives | Keystone (required, or system is dead) | Upgrade slots | When a component is knocked out |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `spindrive` | warp speed → `turns_per_warp`; **efficiency rating** (the global combat bonus, below) | navigator | 0–3 extra turbine/accelerator **pairs** | slower warp + weaker global bonus; no navigator ⇒ stranded (towable to base) |
| `thrusters` | combat/evasion speed → the engine term in the flee roll (§10) | ≥1 burner | burners, turbines, converters, secondaries | fewer burners ⇒ lower combat speed (no burners ⇒ speed halved) ⇒ harder to flee |
| `screens` | the `shields` aspect (separate forward / rear deflection) | secondary | accelerators, converters, radiators | lower deflection chance on the hit hemisphere |
| `main_gun` | spinal primary weapon: damage **and** rate of fire | none (fires with ≥1 component) | accelerator, converter, radiator, secondary, turbine, linkage | slower rate of fire and/or lower damage |

**The shared component vocabulary** (Lightspeed's, minus the dropped blaster/targeter parts): `accelerator`, `converter`, `radiator`, `secondary`, `turbine`, `burner`, `linkage`, `navigator`. The same generic part type is legal in several subsystems, so components are a small, fungible economy — buy, loot, or barter a `converter` and decide where it does the most good. Each component carries a **tech tier** (I–III, keyed to the seller species' `tech_level`): a tier-III `accelerator` from a deep-space overlord outperforms a tier-I one in the same slot. A subsystem's derived aspect is a config-defined formula over its filled slots and their tiers; the old flat per-aspect **cap emerges naturally** from `(slot count × max installable tier)`, so caps stay hull-defined without a separate number.

**Missiles** are *not* a slotted subsystem but a **consumable munition**: a `missile_rack` device holding an ammo count of homing missiles (a `weapon` with `special=homing`) that **ignore firing arc** — the deliberate counter to enemy hulls built around a deadly spinal/`ahead` gun, at the cost of being finite and needing resupply. (Point-defense is intentionally **not** modeled — there is no blaster-turret bay — so enemy missiles are answered by screens and speed alone, keeping the weapon set to Main Gun + Missiles + Screens.)

**Global engine bonus (system coupling).** Rather than Lightspeed's per-converter web, the Spindrive's **efficiency rating** (its count of active turbine/accelerator pairs, 0–N) yields **one legible buff** applied across the board in combat — a small additive bonus to screen deflection, combat speed, and main-gun damage at once, from a config table modeled on the manual's Spindrive-efficiency chart. The design promise is simple to reason about: *tune your engine and your whole ship improves.*

**Repair (hybrid).** Knocked-out ≠ destroyed. A carried **repair-kit pool** (a small consumable, bought or looted, that competes only lightly for space) lets the player **field-patch** one knocked-out component back to minimal function between rounds or between sectors — enough to limp home. **Full restoration, component swaps, and tier upgrades** happen only at **Stardock or a friendly alien base**, paid in latinum or barter. A part that is *lost* with no kit on hand leaves its slot empty until the ship is based.

**One workbench UI (normative).** Everywhere slotted components are shown — the ship's Engine Room and a starbase's Station tab (§4.2) — the client renders through the single shared `ComponentWorkbench` widget (§11), parameterized by a per-host presentation profile. No screen may own a private slot renderer, and repair/salvage act on an explicit player selection of component and slot, never on a silently chosen first match. When exactly one carried component and one ship slot are selected, the Engine Room shows the changed before/after derived aspects from a read-only server projection: it validates through the real install/swap reducer and derives against the reducer's unapplied ship result, so previewing never mutates state and cannot disagree with the eventual command.

**Config and scope.** A `ship_class` *may* carry a `subsystems` block — a per-subsystem list of slots, each `{slot_id, legal_components, base_component | empty}`. Hulls **without** the block (most NPC alien hulls) keep the flat `aspects` / `armament` / `defenses` form from the table above; localized component damage simply does not apply to them, which keeps alien roster authoring lightweight. The starter player hull (a `Trailblazer`-class) ships **complete but minimal** — every base slot filled, every upgrade slot empty — making it the canonical first upgrade target and tying the engine room directly into the Phase 1→2 progression loop.

### 4.2 Planets: Types, Ownership, and Orbital Starbases

Planets are more than colony pods and descent sites: they are **typed** (what they yield), **ownable** territory (who holds them, and therefore who defends them), and some are guarded by **orbital starbases** that reuse the engine-room component model (§4.1). This section specifies all three layers. They interlock with the alliance/territory spine (§6.3), the economy (§8), exploration (§7), and combat (§10), but the structural model lives here next to the component vocabulary the starbases borrow.

#### Planet types

Adapting Lightspeed's planetary taxonomy (manual §1.4.6) to our canonical commodity trio. Every planet carries a `planet_type` that fixes whether it can be colonized, a per-commodity `yield_profile` multiplier over **Fuel Ore / Organics / Equipment**, and a `habitability` term that caps colonist population and growth. We keep the trio sacred (no fourth commodity): Lightspeed's Metal / Organics / Radioactives / Water are expressed as yield-and-habitability *shaping*, not new goods — radioactive Hot worlds read as Fuel-Ore-rich, metal-rich Cold worlds as Equipment-rich, watery Cool worlds as high-habitability colony sites, and so on.

| `planet_type` | Lightspeed analog | Colonizable | Rich in | Habitability | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `terrestrial_warm` | Warm / organics | yes | Organics | high | prime colony world; rarest terrestrial |
| `terrestrial_cool` | Cool / water | yes | Organics (+ growth) | high | abundant water lifts colonist cap and growth |
| `terrestrial_hot` | Hot / radioactives | yes | Fuel Ore | low | youthful, irradiated systems |
| `terrestrial_cold` | Cold / metals | yes | Equipment | low | frozen, metal-rich rock |
| `jovian` | gas giant | no | Fuel Ore (scoop only) | — | fuel-scoopable, no surface colony |
| `asteroid_belt` | rubble | no | Equipment (raw metal, thin) | — | **spatial feature, not landable** (see below); classic pirate haunt; minable, not colonizable |
| `barren` (`double_star`/dead) | double-star / dead world | no | none | — | scientific / discovery interest only |

`yield_profile` and `habitability` values are config. Type is **band-weighted** at big bang (Core and Hub favor habitable terrestrials; outer bands skew toward uncolonizable and resource-extreme worlds), and it feeds three systems: colonist production (§8), the descent surface-site roll (§7 — a `barren` rock is a poor colony but can hide the best ruins), and what a world makes available for trade and barter. Uncolonizable types still have extraction value — `jovian` worlds permit **fuel-scooping** and `asteroid_belt` worlds **raw mining**, both producing without colonists (and the latter is where space-pirate NPCs base, per the manual).

**Capabilities are keyed by type, not inferred (WP-PR06).** Two per-type predicates gate legality everywhere — `colonizable` (config) and `landable` (config, default true). A **`colonizable`** world is the only kind that holds colonists, colony `stores`, a citadel/treasury/garrison, and an owner; it also gates claim, colony transfer, citadel build, planetary banking, and invasion. A **`landable`** world has a solid surface to descend onto and explore surface sites. **Asteroid belts are the one non-`landable` type**: a belt is a *spatial "world object"*, not a planet in the interaction sense. It is scanned and mined in orbit and may sit in a sector with open-space finds, but it can never be descended onto, colonized, hold colonists/stores, host a citadel, be owned (the big bang leaves every belt unowned, base-less, and colony-less — enforced by `bigbang.populate._normalize_belts` and the idempotent `core.planets.normalize_belt`, so legacy saves converge on re-read), or be terraformed by Genesis (belts are excluded from `genesis.eligible_types`). The surface-site and hostile-homeworld-cache rolls skip belts, and no orbital starbase is hung off one. The fog-safe `PlanetDTO` carries `landable`/`extractable`/`mine_yield` alongside `colonizable` so the TUI renders a belt's orbital/mining view — never the descent/colony/citadel controls — from projected capability, and the reducers reject every colony/descent command on a belt with a specific error. **Player-driven belt mining (PT-30)** is the `MineBelt` command: a turn cost that hauls the belt's orbital yield (Equipment, `planets.asteroid_mining`, clamped to free cargo holds) aboard the ship and emits `BeltMined`. It reuses the same `asteroid_mining` config the owned-world `produce` auto-collect draws on, so the two extraction paths stay balanced by one value; because a belt is always unowned, `MineBelt` is the only route to its output. `belt_mining_yield(planet_type, config)` is the shared pure seam (used by the reducer, `produce`, and the projection).

#### Ownership

A planet's `owner` is one of three kinds — **unowned**, an **alliance**, or the **player** — mirroring the territorial spine of the alliance system (§6.3):

- **Core Space planets are owned by the governing alliance, automatically.** Every planet in sectors 1–10 is held by `Game.core_governing_alliance_id` (the Federation at start) and **re-keys if governance changes hands** (§6.3); Core worlds are never unowned.
- **Alliance-owned** beyond the Core — a world a bloc colonized or was seeded onto (the bloc's **home cluster** just outside the Core, §5/§6.3, plus any outward holdings). Its posture toward an entrant is read through the alliance graph: rival-bloc ships and players are enemies; members and good-standing players are welcome.
- **Player-owned** — a world the player has colonized (BNT colonization, §8) or seized. Player holdings are the deep-space footholds the exploration loop builds toward.
- **Unowned** — wild, unclaimed worlds: the frontier's prize, free to colonize or mine.

**Unowned density rises with distance from the Core.** Ownership is band-weighted at big bang: the Core is wholly governor-owned, the Hub heavily alliance-owned, and the fraction of **unowned** planets climbs monotonically through Frontier → Deep → Void — so pushing outward rewards the player not only with rarer discoveries but with *claimable territory*. A world inhabited by an **unaligned** (wild-card) species keeps `owner = none` but carries an `inhabited_by_species_id`: it is not alliance territory, and if it is defended at all, that defense keys off the inhabiting species' **effective disposition** toward the player rather than alliance hostility (below). NPC species that belong to a bloc hold their worlds through that bloc (alliance-owned).

#### Colonists

**Colonists are people, not cargo.** They are sapient beings who *choose* whether to join the player's colony — they are never a commodity and are never bought or sold like the Fuel Ore / Organics / Equipment trio. A colonist comes aboard only by being **recruited**: persuaded to sign on, typically for a paid **incentive** (a recruitment bounty), not purchased as an item. Two recruitment sources:

- **Stardock** — a recruitment office where prospective colonists gather; the player pays a per-head incentive (latinum) to enlist willing recruits. The latinum is an inducement to the colonists, not a transaction with a vendor over goods.
- **Inhabited worlds with a positive disposition toward the player** — a friendly-leaning world (alliance member in good standing, or an unaligned species at amicable effective disposition) will let some of its people emigrate to join the player's colony. Hostile or indifferent worlds will not.

Because colonists are beings and not goods, **they do not consume cargo holds.** A ship carries them against a **separate occupancy limit** (`Ship.colonist_capacity`, a per-hull stat — life-support berths, distinct from `holds_total`), so loading colonists never competes with the commodity cargo the trade loop depends on. They are loaded, carried, and settled — `Colonize` moves recruited colonists from the ship's occupancy onto an **unowned** colonizable world, claiming it, and its top-up counterpart `SettleColonists` lands more of them onto a world the player **already owns** (clamped to the berth aboard and the world's remaining habitability, WP-PR07) — but at no point are they priced, conserved, or exchanged as a tradeable commodity. The colonist *production* model below (§8) governs what a settled colony then makes; recruitment governs how the colony is peopled in the first place.

#### Orbital starbases

A planet may carry an **orbital starbase** (`Planet.starbase_id`) — a stationary defensive structure that reuses the engine-room component model (§4.1) **minus the mobility subsystems** (no `spindrive`, no `thrusters`: a starbase neither warps nor maneuvers) and **plus a `fusion_reactor`** that takes the Spindrive's place as the base's power plant and global-efficiency source. Its three subsystems, built from the same shared component vocabulary (§4.1), are:

| Subsystem | Derives | Keystone (or system is dead) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| `fusion_reactor` | the base's **efficiency rating** → one global bonus to screen deflection and main-gun damage (the Spindrive's role) and the power for repair/defense | `converter` | efficiency = count of active turbine/accelerator pairs, as the Spindrive; no converter ⇒ base goes dark |
| `screens` | the base's `shields` aspect (forward / rear deflection) | `secondary` | identical to the ship subsystem |
| `main_gun` | the base's spinal defensive gun: damage and rate of fire | none (fires with ≥1 component) | a fixed orbital weapon |

Because the reactor is built from the same `converter` / `turbine` / `accelerator` / `radiator` / `secondary` parts as a ship's engine room, **components are fungible across ships and bases** — one economy of parts spanning combat salvage, alien barter, and starbase scavenge. Big set-piece bases also carry the flat `armament` / `defenses` extras of any `ship_class` (laser_turret garrison, armour, energy_plates) and may field garrison `fighters` or an escort `fleet`.

**Operational vs. derelict is emergent, not a stored flag.** A starbase has no `condition` field: it is **operational** when its component state can power and defend it (the keystone reactor `converter` present and functional, plus minimal screens and gun) and **derelict** when broken or missing components leave it unable to — exactly the same component-health logic that already governs a battle-damaged ship (§4.1). The big bang exploits this directly: a base it creates at an **owned** planet (Core / alliance / player) is built with intact components (operational — powered, crewed, defending); a base at an **unowned, uninhabited** world is subject to an `is_derelict` roll, and one that comes up derelict is **made so by removing or damaging its components** — an abandoned holding ground down by some earlier age (a hook for the lore/discovery layer, §7). Because derelict-ness is *just missing parts*, nothing about it is special-cased: it cannot defend, and bringing it back to life is ordinary engine-room work.

**Scavenging.** From a **derelict** base — or any base the **player owns** — the player can pull installed components out of the subsystem slots into the hold, exactly the engine-room cannibalize action (§4.1), feeding the same fungible component economy as combat salvage and alien barter. A derelict is a deep-space component cache: stripping it is the quick win.

**Claiming and repairing.** A player who owns the underlying planet can repair a derelict base by installing components (scavenged, bought, or bartered) into its empty / damaged slots until the keystone reactor `converter` and at least minimal screens and gun are live — at which point it is simply **operational again, under the player's ownership** (its `owner` set to the player's `player_id`). A repaired player base becomes a **forward foothold**: a defended holding that (config-gated) offers a subset of Stardock services in deep space — echoing Lightspeed's home-base role and paying off the outward push with logistics. (Phase 3 ships defense + salvage + claim/repair.) **Forward-base services (Phase 5 — implemented, WP53):** an operational, player-owned orbital base offers a config-gated service set (`starbase.services`) — battle-damage repair, component purchase/swap from a Tier-I/II catalog, repair-kit and missile resupply, and access to the player's bank account — each priced at a configured markup over Stardock (frontier convenience costs more). Availability is resolved through a single **service-point** seam shared with Stardock: the same commands work at either provider, only availability and fees differ, and a *friendly alien base* is a future config extension of the same seam rather than a new code path. Movement costs turns, not fuel (Appendix B.4), so "refuel" has no analog here — resupply covers the consumables instead.

**Defense (ownership × alliance hostility).** When a ship enters a sector whose planet carries an **operational** starbase, the base evaluates the entrant's **hostility** toward the base's owner; if hostile, it joins the encounter as a fixed combatant (its `main_gun`, `screens`, garrison fighters, and any escort `fleet`), adding to the planetary system's defense:

- **alliance-owned** base → a hostile entrant is a player aligned with one of the owner's `rival_alliance_ids` (or below an at-war standing threshold), or an NPC ship whose species is rival / at-war by inter-species relations (§6.4). Core-governor bases enforce the Core sanctuary rule (§10) through exactly this mechanism.
- **player-owned** base → a hostile entrant is an NPC ship whose species' **effective disposition** toward the player sits in the hostile band, or a rival-bloc member if the player is aligned.
- **unowned-but-inhabited** (unaligned species, if it has an operational base) → defends against entrants the inhabiting species reads as hostile, on effective disposition alone.
- **derelict / unowned** → no defense.

Defense strength scales with the base's **surviving components** (a battered base defends weakly) and its `fusion_reactor` efficiency bonus — so knocking the reactor or screens offline before pressing an assault matters, exactly as Lightspeed's "kill the escorts and screens first" doctrine (manual §3.2.3). Razing an alliance's planetary base is the same coin-of-diplomacy set-piece as razing any starbase (§10) — it additionally strips the owner of that world's defense and can flip the planet toward unowned and claimable.

**Base-hosted markets (WP78).** Every starbase sector holds a commodity port, and the base **is** the sector's trading post: where the big bang's port draw left a base sector empty, a standard-class port is minted there in a post-pass (`populate._host_markets`, its own sub-RNG salt), and no free-standing port is presented alongside a base — the market lives on the base, keeping the §8 pricing/haggling/order-book machinery unchanged underneath. Access is gated at the trade seam, not the UI: the market is **dark** while the base is derelict (repair-and-claim switches it on — one more payoff of the foothold loop) and **closed** to any player the base's owner counts as an enemy (the same WP40 defense predicate), while a Stardock is sovereign and never base-hosted. **A powered base is not automatically open for business (WP-PR04):** market, hardware, munitions, and bank are gated behind a single `services_operational` predicate — the reactor keystone live *and* surviving-component integrity at or above `starbase.service_integrity_min` (default 0.70). Below that line a battered base still hosts **recovery** (salvage and component repair are never gated — repair needs no prior claim; installing a component never sets ownership) but withholds all customer-facing services until repaired back above the threshold, and an owned base damaged below it closes services immediately and reopens them on repair. The DTO carries the required percentage so the base screen can explain a greyed-out tab rather than merely omitting it. A **player- or corp-owned** host skims a configured commission (`starbase.services.trade_cut_frac`, default 5%) of each *outsider* trade's value out of the port's purse into the owner's bank — rent for holding the local market; owners and corp-mates trade free (you don't pay yourself), alliance/unowned hosts take no cut, and the cut clamps to the purse so it never goes negative (order-book mode only — no purse, no rent).

**Cargo transfer (the colony-supply rail).** The owner (or their corp) may haul goods between the ship's hold and an owned world's **stores**, in-sector, both directions (`TransferCargo` — clamped to what is aboard / the free holds, goods conserved exactly). Aggregate load-all/unload-all uses `BatchTransferCargo`: all accepted commodity rows are reduced and persisted as one atomic command/event group, with shared loading capacity allocated in stable commodity order. This is how a citadel gets built: level costs draw equipment from *stores*, no hull carries a level's worth in one trip, and colony production alone fills stores far too slowly — supply runs in trips are the intended loop (the classic TW colony haul).

**Citadels and planetary combat (Phase 5 — implemented WP54/WP55: citadels, treasury, timed builds, the gun, and the raze→silence→ground-assault invasion ladder with conquest ownership flips).** A colonized, owned world can raise a **citadel** in three cumulative levels, each paid up front in equipment + latinum and completed as a **timed build** measured in colonist-days: progress (`Planet.citadel_progress`) accrues on the planet-growth tick in proportion to colonists, one build open per planet, and an in-progress build is neither lootable nor cancellable — conquest simply inherits it. Level 1 grants a **treasury** (`Planet.treasury`, an interest-free planetary latinum store the owner deposits/withdraws in-sector — its value is location, not yield) and a garrison defense bonus; level 2 a fixed **planetary gun** that joins the sector's defense exactly as an orbital base does; level 3 a **siege shield** under which the world cannot be invaded while its orbital base or planetary gun still stands. Conquest follows a strict ladder: **raze the orbital base** (§10), **silence the gun**, then **ground assault** — the attacker's carried fighters against the planet's garrison (`Planet.fighters`, produced by a colonist allocation share alongside the commodity trio), with odds shaped by citadel level. Victory flips ownership to the invader, captures the treasury, loots stores at salvage fractions, downgrades the citadel one level, and spares a configured fraction of colonists; defeat costs the committed fighters and triggers the full attitude/grudge/alignment consequence rail toward the owner (§6.5, §10). Core worlds cannot be invaded (the deployment-free Core, §10).

## 5. Universe Generation ("Big Bang")

Deterministic from `(seed, config)`. Default 1000 sectors (config 100–5000). Pipeline, synthesizing SectorWars' algorithm note, twclone's bigbang, and ExchangeConflict's motifs:

1. **Cluster pass.** Partition sectors into groups of 5–25; within each group, connect each sector bidirectionally to a random other member, then add extra intra-group edges until average intra-group degree ≈ 2.5. Name each group from a region-name generator (adjective + noun pools, ExchangeConflict-style word lists).
2. **Bridge pass.** How groups interconnect is selected by **`topology_mode`** (config, per game). Two modes ship:
   - **`trunk`** (the original algorithm): a spanning tree over groups rooted at the Core group — each group gets one mandatory bridge to a random earlier group — plus 0–4 extra bridges per group; with probability `one_way_chance` (default 0.15) an extra bridge is directional only. Traffic to the outer bands funnels through the few tree bridges, giving a trunk-and-branches universe of defensible chokepoints.
   - **`expansive`** (the band-lattice web): the groups are stratified into concentric rings (the Core is ring 0; ring membership is seeded), then bridged as a lattice — each ring's groups are wired into a **ring road** (a cycle, so a band can be circled without diving back toward the Core) and each consecutive ring pair is joined by **≥2 radial spokes** on distinct groups, plus a few one-way chords for texture. Cycles-plus-two-spokes make the graph **bridgeless**: no single inter-region warp is a cut edge, so every group keeps two edge-disjoint paths to the Core. Because spoke demand is only ~2 per ring boundary (not per group), the 10-sector Core is never swamped. Each band becomes a widening ring-lattice with many routes and no single-bridge chokepoints — an expansive, branching frontier rather than a trunk.
   Both modes must satisfy the same step-8 invariants; the mode changes the *shape* of danger (chokepoint ambushes vs. open flanks), not the rules. **`expansive` is the recommended default for new games** — its open, branching frontier is the design's stated intent — but the shipped default stays `trunk` until the WP22 config epoch flips it (so the flip and its one golden-master regeneration land together, not piecemeal).
3. **Motif pass.** Inject configured counts of *tunnels* (chains of length 4–9 grafted at one end — twclone default ~15), *deadends* (1–2 sector stubs), and *rings* (cycles of 3/5/7/9, ExchangeConflict weights).
4. **Core Space carve.** Sectors 1–10 become the **Core Space**: a fully interlinked neighborhood around sector 1 (Terra), protected (no attacks, no fighter/mine deployment) under its governing alliance, with guaranteed exits to the wider graph (twclone's `ensure_fedspace_exit` analog). The game's `core_governing_alliance_id` is set to the roster's `initial_core_governor` (the Federation by default), and `Player.alliance_id` is seeded to that same alliance.
5. **Distance bands.** Compute every sector's warp-hop distance from sector 1 and bucket into config-defined bands (e.g. Hub 0–5, Frontier 6–12, Deep 13–20, Void 21+). Bands drive alien species placement, discovery rarity, and encounter danger throughout the game. Because the `expansive` mode's ring-road lattice yields a **different hop-distance profile** than the trunk spanning tree, band thresholds are **per-mode config**: each mode's band list lives in its own block under `bigbang.topology.<mode>` (`topology.trunk.bands`, `topology.expansive.bands`, …; the `expansive` windows retuned so all four bands stay populated), resolved by `topology_mode` at generation via `active_topology()`. The same `topology.<mode>` block also carries that mode's own generation parameters — for `trunk`, its `bridges_min`/`bridges_max` extra-bridge range; the shared cluster/degree/one-way knobs stay flat under `bigbang`. Every mode's band list must name the same bands in the same order — only the hop windows differ.
6. **Alliance home clusters.** Each non-governing alliance in the drawn cast (§6) is given a compact **home cluster** — a small interlinked group of regions (config: default 3–6 sectors, always **smaller than the 10-sector Core**) carved into the **Hub and inner-Frontier** bands, **never adjacent to the Core Space** and **never directly warp-linked to another alliance's cluster**. The sectors *between* the clusters — and between every cluster and the Core — are left as **neutral lanes**: unowned, alliance-free navigable space (the ordinary cluster/bridge graph from passes 1–2) that the player threads to pass from the Core outward **without being forced through any bloc's territory**. Each cluster's regions are tagged with their `controlling_alliance_id` (§4); its worlds are seeded **alliance-owned** and its bloc's friendly-band members placed there (step 7). A `covets_core` bloc's cluster is the staging ground for the Phase-5 seize-the-Core endgame (§6.3). The generator guarantees **at least one all-neutral path from the Core to each outer band**, so the frontier is always reachable without trespass.
7. **Populate.** Stardock (Class 9) placed 2–5 hops from the Core Space. Standard ports at ~45% sector density with the terminal-space class distribution (20/20/20/10/10/10/5/5) and initial stock `randint(200, 2000)` scaled by port size; port density thins in the outer bands (deep space is wild, not commercial). Planets seeded in ~25% of sectors, each assigned a **band-weighted `planet_type`** (§4.2: Core and Hub favor habitable terrestrials; outer bands skew toward uncolonizable and resource-extreme worlds) and an **owner** (§4.2): Core planets are owned by the initial governing alliance unconditionally, Hub planets are heavily alliance/species-owned, and the **unowned fraction rises monotonically** through Frontier → Deep → Void. **Orbital starbases** are placed per config and per each owner's `starbase_policy`: a base at an owned planet is built with intact components, while a base at an **unowned, uninhabited** world gets an `is_derelict` roll — if it comes up derelict, the generator effects that by **removing or damaging the base's components** (so a derelict is just a damaged base, trivially repaired by refilling its slots, §4.2). After alien home regions exist, every ordinary port and orbital base receives a seeded **builder `archetype_id`** drawn from the active configured species roster; a regional controller or owning species/alliance supplies it when possible, while unaligned structures select deterministically from roster archetypes. It never changes on capture or repair. **Aliens:** a seeded subset is drawn from the configured species roster (Section 6) — not every roster member need appear — and each instantiated species draws its base disposition from a config-bounded spread around its roster center, then is assigned home regions. A roster species flagged **`singular_entity`** (§6.1, §7) is special-cased: it is **always drawn**, fielded as **exactly one instance** spawned in a deep-band sector, excluded from the per-band contact guarantees, satellite clustering, and the Stardock/Core placements, and a matching hidden Legendary `entity` discovery row is reserved for its first contact to collect. The Core Space and the **neutral lanes** around it receive only members of the initial governing alliance (the Federation by default) whose realized disposition sits in the friendly band, keeping the player's home approaches safe; each **other alliance's home cluster** (step 6) is filled with its own bloc's friendly-band members and its worlds set alliance-owned, while unaligned species — and any rival blocs' outward holdings — claim territory in the outer bands, with homeworlds at tunnel endpoints (twclone) and mean threat rating rising (and mean disposition falling) with band. Hub-band alliance clusters are populated from **friendly-band** draws like the governing alliance's, so the Hub stays innately peaceable: a cluster's danger to the player is **alliance-political** (rival-bloc standing, activated once the player aligns against it, §6.3/§10), not low disposition. **Discoveries:** every sector and planet rolls on its band's rarity table (Section 7); planets additionally roll surface sites (ruins, artifacts, ancient tech, crashed ships).
8. **Validate.** Assert: single strongly-reachable component from sector 1 (treating one-ways correctly); max warps per sector ≤ 6 (TW2002 canon); Stardock reachable; at least one profitable port-pair (opposed classes, e.g. BBS↔SSB) within 5 hops of the Core Space so new players can earn; per-region port balance within tolerance; all Hub-band regions host only friendly-band species (none below the friendly disposition band present at generation), while the Core Space and the neutral home lanes around it host only members of the initial governing alliance (no rival-bloc species adjacent to the Core); **each non-governing alliance in the cast owns exactly one home cluster, each smaller than the Core, none adjacent to the Core and none directly warp-linked to another cluster, with at least one all-neutral (alliance-free) path connecting the Core Space to every outer band** (the frontier reachable without transiting any bloc's territory); mean discovery rarity/value strictly increasing across bands; mean alien disposition non-increasing across bands; at least one friendly-disposition contact point per band so deep explorers can resupply and barter; **all Core Space planets owned by the initial governing alliance** (none unowned); the **unowned-planet fraction is non-decreasing across distance bands**; **any derelict starbase (missing/damaged keystone components) sits on an unowned, uninhabited planet**, and every starbase on an owned planet has intact components (operational); at least one **habitable** (terrestrial warm/cool) world reachable within the Hub so early colonization is possible; **all invariants hold under both `topology_mode`s**, and in `expansive` mode **no inter-region warp is a cut edge** — the graph is bridgeless, so every group keeps ≥2 edge-disjoint paths to the Core (the no-chokepoint property); **exactly one `singular_entity` instance exists, never placed in the Core**. Regenerate with a perturbed sub-seed on failure (bounded retries, then error).

A dev tool `edge bigbang --render DIR` writes a self-contained interactive web inspector (inline SVG + vanilla JS, no external assets) of the generated topology: sectors drawn on their §5.1 radial embedding, each sector's ports/planets/discoveries/species as caption symbols, one-way vs two-way warps distinguished (arrowed vs plain), dotted Core / cluster / distance-band outlines (each toggleable), an internal/spatial id toggle, zoom/pan with a fixed-size title and legend, and **edge editing** (click two nodes to add a directed warp, click an edge twice to delete) exportable as JSON for offline analysis of topology-algorithm changes. `--dump-json PATH` emits just the payload. (An in-TUI map debugger complements it.)

### 5.1 Spatial sector numbering (a derived, UI-only secondary id)

**Status: implemented as a dual-id model.** Internal sector ids stay a flat `1..N` assigned by the cluster pass — they remain the **authoritative key** across the §4 data model, the runtime adjacency, the SQLite command/event log, the golden-master state hashes, and every test fixture. A flat opaque id is faithful to TW2002 (which numbers up to 5000 sectors as a directed graph, where "efficiently navigating the graph is much of the game") but is exactly the trial-and-error the playtest feedback objects to *as a player-facing number*. So rather than renumber anything internally, the game derives a second, **spatial display id** per sector (`edge/bigbang/numbering.py::assign_spatial_ids`) and shows *only that* to the player. This sidesteps the cutover risk entirely: nothing authored or persisted changes; the spatial id is a pure projection. (A full cutover that made spatial ids the internal key was the original idea, rejected as needless risk — see the dual-id note below.)

**Research basis.** Distance-from-core on the primary axis follows **Star Trek / Federation coordinates** (origin at a core beacon). A multi-level *region-prefix + local-ordinal* name follows **Elite Dangerous** boxel naming (`Sector + grid cell + ordinal`) and **Outer Reaches** letter-number subregions. Wayfinding research on hierarchical navigation graphs (people plan **coarsely between regions, finely within**; regional labels cut cognitive load and aid recall) endorses *region-prefix + locally-sequential-within-region*, complemented by the coarse directional cue already shipped — the warp **gravity arrows** (`<<` / `--` / `>>`, §11/WP-C).

**Scheme — a multi-level, gapped, band-monotone integer.** A sector id is composed of three positional fields, most-significant first:

1. **Band digit** — the distance-band index (Hub < Frontier < Deep < Void), so a numerically larger id always means *farther from the Core*. One decimal digit (≤ 9 bands).
2. **Region prefix** — shared by every sector in a region (cluster). Regions are numbered **within their band**, ordered by representative (minimum-hop) distance.
3. **Local ordinal** — the sector's position within its region, ordered by hop distance then id. For a **tunnel** motif region (§5 step 3) this lays the chain out **sequentially** (Halaf 10, 11, 12, 13), signalling a linear path; ordinaries are simply grouped.

Encoded positionally as `new_id = (band_index + 1)·band_stride + region_local·region_stride + ordinal`, with field widths derived from the actual counts (so a 1000-sector universe yields compact 4–5 digit ids — e.g. `10101` for Terra — and larger universes widen gracefully). The mapping is **gapped** (unfilled slots leave holes — the feedback explicitly allows this) and a **pure function of topology** (no RNG; determinism comes from the inputs).

**Worked example** — actual `assign_spatial_ids` output for a 120-sector universe (here ids read `B RR OO`: a one-digit band, a two-digit region-within-band, a two-digit ordinal):

- **Terra anchors the Core.** Old sector `1` → **`10101`** = band 1 (Hub), region 01, ordinal 01 — the lowest id in the universe.
- **Region-atomic prefix + local ordering.** The whole Core cluster shares the `101` prefix and numbers sequentially by hop distance: old `1, 2, 4, 9, 10, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8` → `10101, 10102, 10103, 10104, 10105, 10106, 10107, 10108, 10109, 10110`. One glance at the prefix says "these ten are one place."
- **Band-monotone, with tunnel-style ordinals.** A Frontier cluster takes band digit `2` — `20101 … 20116` — so *every* Frontier id sorts above *every* Hub id (`1xxxx`). Within it the ordinals climb with hop distance (`20101` at 6 hops → `20116` at 11 hops), laying a linear chain out in reading order.
- **Region atomicity vs. strict per-sector monotonicity (the decision, made visible).** Old sector `46` sits one hop *into* the Frontier (6 hops out), but it belongs to a cluster whose representative sector is in the Hub — so it keeps that cluster's Hub prefix → **`10604`** (band 1, region 06), *not* a `2xxxx` id. Its exact outward direction is still conveyed by the WP-C gravity arrows; the shared prefix keeps the straddling cluster legible as a single region rather than splitting it across two band prefixes.

**The region/monotonicity tension, and the decision.** A cluster of 5–25 internally-connected sectors can physically **straddle a band boundary**, so "every region shares one prefix" and "strict per-sector higher-id-is-farther" cannot both hold. We resolve in favour of **region atomicity**, the larger legibility win: each region is assigned a single **home band** (the band of its representative min-hop sector) and all its sectors share that `band+region` prefix even if a few sit one hop into the neighbouring band. Monotonicity therefore holds at **region granularity** (regions, ordered by representative hop, increase outward) rather than per-sector; per-sector direction is still exact via the gravity arrows. The alternative — splitting a straddling region across two band prefixes to keep strict per-sector monotonicity — is rejected because it breaks the shared-prefix promise that makes the scheme readable.

**Validator invariants** (pinned by `tests/test_numbering.py` and asserted on the wired-in map in `tests/test_bigbang.py`): the mapping is a **bijection** over all sectors — no collisions, so it is reversible for player input; **region-atomic** (all of a region's sectors share one `band+region` prefix); **band-monotone** (sorting sectors by id yields non-decreasing band; each sector's band digit equals its region's representative band); and **deterministic** (identical inputs → identical mapping). Terra (sector 1) anchors the lowest id. The bijection is what lets the one place a player *types* a number (the travel prompt) translate the spatial id back to an internal id.

**The dual-id model (how it's wired, and why no migration is needed).** Spatial ids are **derived, not authored**, so they need not touch persistence at all. `assign_spatial_ids(groups, core_hops, bands)` runs once at the end of `generate` (after bands and `core_hops` exist) into a **runtime cache** `UniverseState.spatial_ids` — exactly like `core_hops`/`adjacency`, which `state_hash` already excludes. The cache is recomputed identically on every load (`rebuild` re-runs `generate` from the seed), so it never needs saving. The id is surfaced **only at the `server/session.py` projection boundary**: the DTOs carry both `sector_id` (internal — the click/command payload and key) and a `display_id` (spatial — what the TUI renders); the warp labels, sector title, sidebar neighbours, port/Stardock headers, map "you", the Stardock signpost, and the "Warp to Sector N" log line all show `display_id`. The single reverse path — the travel prompt, where the player types a number — calls `GameService.resolve_display_id` to map the spatial id back to internal before issuing `TravelTo`. Consequence: the command log, codec, replay, and golden-master hashes are **unchanged**, and the legacy `Warp`/`TravelTo` payloads stay internal. If spatial ids were ever wanted as the *internal* key (not currently planned), that would be the risky cutover the dual-id model deliberately avoids — a `config_version` bump regenerating golden masters, remapping sectors/`warps_out`/ports/planets/regions and the seeded `explored_sectors`/`entered_from`.

**Spatial embedding (a third derived cache, giving direction).** The warp graph is purely topological — sectors have no coordinates — so the only directional signal is the `core_hops` scalar, which can say a warp goes toward/away from the Core but not *which way* two "deeper" exits diverge. To give the main-screen **nav rose** (§11) a real sense of direction, `generate` computes a stable 2D position per sector into a **third runtime cache** `UniverseState.sector_pos` (`edge/bigbang/embedding.py::compute_embedding`), sitting on the exact same rail as `core_hops`/`spatial_ids`: derived from topology at generation, **excluded from `state_hash`**, never persisted, recomputed identically on every `rebuild`. It is a **radial tree fan** — radius = the sector's Core hop distance (so sector 1 is pinned to the origin and the in/out intuition is preserved), angle from a balanced BFS-tree fan sized by subtree leaf count, so sibling warps into different sub-regions get distinct bearings. A single-parent BFS fan is blind to cross-links, which the `expansive` topology (§5 step 2) has in abundance — so each sector's angle is assigned from the **mean of all its min-hop parents' angles**, not one arbitrary tree parent (still a single stdlib O(n) pass), keeping the nav rose truthful on a lattice. It is deliberately **not** `nx.spring_layout`: `bigbang` is `mypy --strict` (networkx ships no stubs) and spring_layout would drag in both networkx and scipy — dependencies we avoid entirely (§15); a force-directed relaxation is also O(n²) per iteration — prohibitive for a 1000-sector universe generated interactively and re-run on every golden-master `rebuild` — whereas the fan is one O(n) pass. Uses only its own `random.Random(seed)` for tie-break jitter, never the game command-stream RNG (§3). Exposed only at the `server/session.py` boundary as **bearings** (fog-safe — a direction reveals no hidden contents): `WarpDTO.bearing` (to each neighbour) and `SectorDTO.core_bearing` (home direction). Because it is outside `state_hash`, any float variation can never affect `(seed, command log)` replay — at worst the picture shifts.

## 6. Alien Species and Alliances

The universe is inhabited by alien species, generated at big bang from a config-defined roster of archetypes and seeded into regions of the warp graph. Alien species replace the single faceless NPC faction of classic TW2002 — the Cabal/Ferengi lineage survives as the most hostile-leaning archetypes and as the friendly traders who keep the economy circulating. The roster format and the species parameters below were stress-tested against a seventeen-species reference catalogue (the Lightspeed Hyades-cluster roster) to confirm they can express a real, idiosyncratic cast — three rival alliances over a field of wild cards, each species with one memorable systemic hook and its own voiced, standing-keyed conversation (§6.7) — purely as data.

**Disposition.** An alien species is not simply *friendly* or *hostile*. Each species carries a **disposition** — a real number on a continuous scale from `0.0` (utterly hostile) to `1.0` (warmly friendly) — which is its innate baseline stance toward outsiders. The roster skews peaceable (default mean disposition above the midpoint, config-tunable), so amicable species outnumber dangerous ones, but most species live in the broad middle: wary, transactional peoples who will trade yet bristle when provoked. Two configurable thresholds carve the scale into descriptive bands used by generation, prose, and UI labels — a *hostility threshold* (default 0.35) below which a species reads as "hostile," and an *amity threshold* (default 0.65) above which it reads as "friendly" — but behavior interpolates continuously along the scale rather than switching at any single line. The Core Space and the neutral lanes around it are populated exclusively by friendly-band members of the alliance that governs the Core (the Federation at generation, §6.3), while every other bloc holds its own friendly-band **home cluster** elsewhere in the Hub and inner Frontier (§5, §6.3) — so the whole Hub reads as innately peaceable, and any menace there is alliance-political rather than disposition-driven. The likelihood of low-disposition species rises with distance from the Core Space, so genuinely hostile territory emerges in the Frontier band and deepens outward.

**Effective disposition.** A species' base disposition is adjusted per player by an *attitude offset* — raised by trading and doing favors, lowered by attacking the species' ships — yielding an **effective disposition** clamped to `[0.0, 1.0]`. Effective disposition is the single quantity that drives every interaction: the probability that an encounter opens with violence rather than greeting, the prices and barter terms an alien species offers, and which technology tiers it will unlock. A player can thus thaw a wary species into a trading partner, or sour a friendly one into an enemy, by their conduct — no species is permanently fixed at a binary stance.

**Tech level.** Each species has a tech level (1–10) expressed in two ways: how fast its ships travel (the engine speed of its hulls — which for hostile-leaning species also makes them harder to outrun) and what technology upgrades it can offer the player. Species disposed to deal will sell or barter upgrades to ship aspects — engine tunings, shield arrays, sensor suites, cloaking modules, hold expansions, armaments — priced in gold-pressed latinum or traded against artifacts and recovered technology from the player's discoveries. Higher-tech species offer better goods but demand rarer barter; an alien species' tech level loosely correlates with distance from the Core Space, so the best upgrades require traveling to, and surviving, deep space.

**Threat and interception.** Every species carries a *threat rating* (damage dealt per combat round) and an *interception rating* (how effectively it prevents a fleeing player from escaping), but how readily it brings them to bear scales with effective disposition: the chance an encounter turns violent rises as effective disposition falls toward `0.0`, and a warmly disposed species will almost never open fire. Among the hostile-leaning species, encounter frequency is inversely proportional to threat — the deadliest aliens are the rarest (encounter weights per band live in config alongside the roster). Flee resolution is specified in Section 10; as a hard invariant, the player's escape probability never drops below a configured floor (default 10%), no matter how heavily damaged the ship is.

**Attitude.** The per-player attitude offset described above is a simple scalar — raised by trading and favors, lowered by aggression — that shifts effective disposition and thereby gates access to a species' best technology tiers. Full diplomacy trees are out of scope for v1.

**Configurable roster.** The set of alien species in play is itself a configuration choice, not a fixed cast. Each game is generated against a named **roster file** — a versioned config listing candidate species with their names, archetypes, base dispositions, tech curves, threat/interception tables, encounter weights, and barter preferences. The big bang draws from this roster rather than instantiating it wholesale, so **no two universe generations need be the same**: a given seed populates only a subset of the roster (some species simply do not appear in a given universe), and an entirely different game can be built from a different source roster (a "Federation classic" roster, a "deep hostile rim" roster, a total-conversion roster of original species) without touching code. Selection is seeded and deterministic — `(seed, roster, config)` reproduces the same cast in the same regions — but varies freely across seeds.

**Per-generation disposition variance.** A species' base disposition in the roster is a *center*, not a fixed value. At big bang each instantiated species draws its actual base disposition from a config-bounded spread around that center (default ±a small variance, tunable per species and globally), so the same species can be warier in one universe and warmer in another. Variance is applied through the seeded RNG and is still subject to all placement invariants — the Core Space and the Hub band only ever receive draws that land in the friendly band (Section 5), and the validator's "mean disposition non-increasing across bands" check is asserted on the *realized* dispositions of the actual cast, not the roster centers. This keeps every generation recognizable yet distinct, and lets the same roster yield a gentle galaxy or a tense one depending on the seed.

### 6.1 Species parameter catalogue

Disposition is the spine, but a species is not reducible to one number. The roster entry for each species carries a structured parameter set (mirrored in the `AlienSpecies` data model, Section 4) that turns a flavor paragraph into game logic. Each parameter below is config, drives a specific subsystem, and is illustrated with the reference-roster exemplar that motivated it:

| Parameter | Type / range | Drives | Exemplar |
|---|---|---|---|
| `disposition_center` + `disposition_variance` | 0.0–1.0 center + spread | base stance; per-generation draw | every species |
| `alliance_id` + `alliance_role` | ref + leader/member/aspirant/none | bloc diplomacy (§6.3) | leads bloc / aspires to join / wild card |
| `tech_level` | 1–10 | hull travel speed *and* the upgrade tiers it will sell/barter | high-tech overlords vs. low-tech fodder |
| `threat_rating` | damage per combat round | combat damage output | heavy capital warships |
| `interception_rating` | 0.0–1.0 | anti-flee term in escape math (§10) | fast pursuers |
| `threat_tier` | fearsome / worthy / feeble / special | encounter pacing + dossier label; *decoupled from `threat_rating`* | a tiny hull that is "lethal one-on-one"; a species "feared out of all proportion to its stats" |
| `combatant` | bool | whether an encounter can become combat at all | pure non-combatants (info brokers, judges, wrecked-pod traders) |
| `trade_posture` | open / earn / goods_only / barter / alliance_gated / circuit_gated / refuses | whether and how trade opens | warlike race that trades only after a fight; a faction that refuses trade entirely |
| `treaty_mode` | open / conditional / prove_intent / alliance_gated / home_planet_only / none / superfluous | whether/how a lasting treaty is offered | "must visit our home planet"; "peace reads as war, no treaty"; "treaty is superfluous, we never fight" |
| `memory_model` | normal / none / never_forgets | how attitude offset persists between encounters | a will-less species that responds identically every time regardless of history; a species that "never forgives, never forgets" |
| `betrayal_model` | recoverable / permanent | the attitude floor after the player attacks/fails them | one betrayal → permanent irreversible hatred |
| `befriend_price` | task list (serve / obey / prove / pay / purge) | what raises attitude and unlocks treaties/tech | "destroy these starbases"; "bring us data"; "prove good conduct over time"; "obey escalating demands" |
| `signature_mechanic` | hook id + params (§6.2) | the species' one unique systemic effect | trojan-gift, reprogram-unlock, influence-gate, morality-judge, … |
| `pack_behavior` + `escort` | solo / escorted / swarm / family_group / colony, + composition | how an encounter group is spawned | "warship escorted by three fighters"; "fast fighters in numbers"; family groups; colonial swarms |
| `fleet` | list of `ship_class` ids | which hulls (and starbase) the species fields | a species with four warship classes; a species with no warships at all |
| `starbase_policy` | none / homeworld / territorial / secret / nomadic_holding | placement of the species' base(s) | ship-dwelling nomads with no base; several secret hard-to-find bases; a single defended homeworld |
| `singular_entity` | bool (default false) | fielded as **exactly one roaming, dialogue-only instance per universe** — always drawn, no satellites, outside band guarantees; it *is* the Entity discovery (§7) | the Concordance — a bodiless judge met as a voice, not a ship |
| `persona` | style key | prose/dialogue voice + the inherited generic `dialogue_pack` (§6.7) | inverted Yoda-like syntax; concept-fragment telepathy; archaic biblical cadence; mangled translator garble |
| `dialogue_pack` | context→conditional line-entry pools (or inherits `persona`) | the encounter/contact **conversation text** (§6.7) | standing-keyed greetings; voice-correct combat taunts; the dossier narration of other species |
| `attitude_gain_rate` / `attitude_loss_rate` | scalars | how fast favors raise, and aggression lowers, the attitude offset | easily-cowed flatterers vs. implacable grudge-keepers |

The point is that two species with *identical* disposition and threat can still play completely differently because their `signature_mechanic`, `trade_posture`, `memory_model`, `befriend_price`, `persona`, and `dialogue_pack` (§6.7) differ — which is exactly how a small roster generates large diplomatic combinatorics.

### 6.2 Signature mechanics

The most reusable idea in the reference catalogue is **one memorable systemic hook per species** rather than uniform stat-blocks. We model this as a registry of named **mechanic hooks**: a species' `signature_mechanic` field names a hook and supplies parameters; the hook is implemented once in `core.mechanics` (a pure registry keyed by `KNOWN_SIGNATURE_HOOKS`) and is data-configured per species. Each hook is a **pure** `(MechanicContext) -> MechanicResult`: it audits the player/species state and the hook's `params` and returns a ladder **stage** (persisted in `Player.species_arcs[roster_id]` under a reserved key, surfaced to selection as the `sig_stage` fact, WP30/§6.7), transient selection facts, and **bounded effects** the `Converse` reducer applies and reports through the existing WP27 events (an attitude offset shift, an alignment/experience nudge, a latinum drop, or forming a grudge) — no hook mutates state or emits events, so the whole thing is replay-safe and layer-clean. A hook is delivered **through dialogue**: an authored `choices` reply transitions into the species' `sig.*` prompt (a reserved context namespace, like `branch.*`), and the reducer runs the hook *before* speaking that line, so the verdict/response line can gate on the `sig_stage` the audit just set. The first four hooks (`morality_judge`, `literalist`, `flee_drop`, `influence_gate`) land in WP33; the transactional remainder in WP37 (an unimplemented hook is inert — the sig line simply speaks with no effect). The built-in hook types (extensible by config) are:

- **`trojan_gift`** — a gift or a request to "lower shields / take this aboard" seeds a delayed harmful payload. Params: `trigger` (accept-gift / lower-shields), `delay`, `effect` (bomb, parasitic infestation that occupies a hold/engine slot, …), `removable_by` (which service or species can purge it). *Counter-mechanic:* a different species may sell removal.
- **`reprogram_unlock`** — installing an item flips another faction's `trade_posture`. Params: `item`, `source_species`, `target_species`, `new_posture`, `side_effect` (e.g. joining an alliance *disables* it — a genuine either/or).
- **`influence_gate`** — the species can forbid being attacked and/or compel player actions while in contact. Params: `cannot_attack_unbidden` (bool), `compel` (forced responses), `breakthrough` (going in "hard and fast" can beat it). Treaties may require travel to a named home planet.
- **`morality_judge`** — a non-trading entity (typically the roaming `singular_entity`, §7) that audits the player's cumulative conduct and dispenses outcomes. Params: `audited_metrics` (aggression, genocide, being-wronged, virtue), `rewards` (blessing, "purge the cluster" of an oppressor), `punishments` (forced battles, colony curse). Fields no ships; defends itself supernaturally.
- **`escalating_demand`** — befriending opens a ladder of mounting demands (donate, destroy named target, surrender your own base); comply and standing holds, **fail once and `betrayal_model=permanent` triggers**. Params: `demand_ladder`, `fail_consequence`.
- **`literalist`** — `memory_model=none`; responses are keyed only to the player's conversational *approach* via a `keyword_map`, ignoring history. Certain offers (e.g. "peace") can be misread as hostile and trigger combat. Params: `keyword_map`.
- **`contract_kill`** — pays resources/goods for razing the starbases of *named* rival species so the patron can move in. Params: `targets`, `reward`, `binding` (trading creates an obligation; betrayal is punished), `redemption` (a path back after refusal).
- **`coordinate_broker`** — a predatory species that pays goods for the coordinates of undefended worlds (it then preys on them). Params: `reward`.
- **`passage_broker`** — sells information and/or special transit (e.g. moving the ship through time) in exchange for goods or the player's home base. Params: `offer`, `price`.
- **`flee_drop`** — `pack_behavior` weak species that flees immediately on contact, dropping collectable cargo packets. Params: `drop_table`.

Combat-side specials are expressed as the `special` field on a **`weapon`** (§4): `engine_flux` (negates the player's engines — counter is to cut engines and drift), `proton_stalker_pod` (autonomous homing platforms), `neutron_spark`/`blaster`/`homing`, etc.; and as exotic **`defense`** types (`energy_plates`, `screens`). Hooks and weapon/defense specials are the seam where a roster expresses personality without code changes.

### 6.3 Alliances

Species are grouped into **alliances** — named blocs declared in the roster. An alliance is config (the `Alliance` entity, §4): a banner concept, a `leader_species`, a member list, a `posture`, a set of `rival_alliance_ids` it is mutually hostile with, and an `admission_price`. Species outside every alliance are **unaligned wild cards**, each pursuing its own agenda. The reference roster's three mutually-hostile blocs over nine wild cards is the canonical shape, but the count and membership are entirely data-driven.

**Each alliance holds a home cluster.** Where the governing alliance holds the Core, every *non-governing* alliance is seeded at big bang with its own compact **home cluster** of worlds in the Hub and inner-Frontier bands (§5) — smaller than the Core, never adjacent to it, and separated from the Core and from rival clusters by **neutral navigable lanes** of unowned space. A cluster is the bloc's territorial anchor: its regions carry the bloc's `controlling_alliance_id`, its worlds are alliance-owned (§4.2) and defended by any orbital starbases there, its friendly-band members live there, and its `admission_price` tasks and rival-razing set-pieces play out around it. The neutral lanes are the player's early freedom: the frontier is always reachable by threading the empty space between clusters, so committing to no bloc never walls the player in. But because the cluster is friendly-band by *disposition*, its space is peaceful only until the player aligns against the bloc — joining a rival turns that cluster hostile on sight (§10), re-keying which near-home routes are safe. A `covets_core` bloc's home cluster is the forward base from which it can be championed into seizing the Core itself (Phase 5).

**The Federation is one alliance among many — it just starts in charge of the Core Space.** No alliance is privileged in the data model; the Federation is an ordinary `Alliance` record. What gives it its starting prominence is *governance of the Core Space* — the protected central region of sectors 1–10 (§5). Governance is a single piece of mutable game state, `Game.core_governing_alliance_id`, initialized at big bang from the roster's `initial_core_governor` setting (the Federation in the default roster). The governing alliance's friendly-band members are the species seeded into the Core and the neutral home lanes around it (every other bloc holds its own home cluster elsewhere in the Hub, §5, §6.3), its forces keep the peace there, and **the player begins as a member of that governing alliance** (`Player.alliance_id` starts as the Federation) — which is exactly what makes the Core Space a safe home to bank, trade, and resupply in at the start of a game.

**Core sanctuary follows the governor, not the Federation.** Whether the Core Space is safe for the player is decided by the player's standing with **whichever alliance currently governs it** (§10), not by any fixed Federation status. At game start those are the same alliance, so the Core is home. But because alliance membership is exclusive, **joining any other bloc resigns the player's membership in the governing alliance**; and aligning with an alliance that the governor counts among its `rival_alliance_ids` makes the player an enemy in the Core's eyes — **the Core Space is no longer safe to enter**, its forces engaging the player on sight until that allegiance is given up and amends are made. Aligning with a bloc the governor merely tolerates forfeits the member's standing without making the Core outright hostile. This is the sharpest "every choice closes a door" decision in the game: the deepest alien tech may lie behind a bloc that costs the player their homeland.

**Governance can change hands.** Control of the Core Space is not frozen at the Federation. An alliance flagged `covets_core` can be brought to power — by the player (championing a rival bloc and dismantling the incumbent's Core presence as that bloc's admission price) or by NPC events (a `covets_core` alliance's own expansion, or an `internal_rival` usurping its alliance and turning it outward). When governance flips, `Game.core_governing_alliance_id` updates and everything keyed to it re-evaluates: who is seeded/welcome in the Core, whose law runs there, and — crucially — whether the *player* is now welcome or hunted, on the same standing rule above. A player who fought to install their own alliance inherits a safe Core under a new flag; a player caught on the wrong side of a takeover can lose a home they never attacked. **Implemented (Phase 5, WP49–WP52):** the flip reducer, the player-championed petition path, the NPC governance/leadership-intrigue crons, and the aftermath surfacing (the game screen names the governor and the player's Core status — safe / unwelcome / hunted; a hunted player is turned away at the Core Stardock, denying every dock-gated service). Phase 3 shipped the static-governor case (Federation governs throughout) as the on-ramp.

**Seizure mechanics (Phase 5 — implemented, WP49–WP52).** A governance flip is **one atomic state change** however it is triggered: `Game.core_governing_alliance_id` updates and, in the same reduction, every Core planet and its orbital starbase re-keys to the new governor, members of the deposed alliance whose sectors are now illegal are **evicted** to the nearest legal sector (deterministically — BFS, ties by lowest sector id, no RNG), and the Stardock hub's standing contacts re-evaluate against the new Core-welcome set. Core law, species occupancy, and base defense need **no** re-keying — they already read the live governor. Two triggers exist. *Player-championed:* a member of a `covets_core` bloc completes its `core_seizure` price — a `befriend_price`-style task ledger plus razing a configured number of the incumbent's Core-planet starbases and a latinum fee — then petitions the bloc's membership gate to install it as governor. *NPC events:* a low-probability daily roll (config-gated, drawn from a salted maintenance sub-RNG sequenced by `Game.governance_seq`) that can fire only while a `covets_core` bloc's home-cluster bases stand intact **and** the incumbent's Core-planet bases have fallen below a configured threshold — a flip never comes from nowhere. A related **leadership-intrigue** event (same cron, separate probability) promotes an alliance's `internal_rival_species_id` to leader and may, per config, turn the usurped bloc's ambitions toward the Core. A flip never rewrites the player's membership or standings; welcome and safety re-derive from the standing rule above.

**Alliances are mutually exclusive for the player.** The player may join at most one alliance at a time (`Player.alliance_id`), and per-alliance standing is tracked separately (`Player.alliance_standing`). Joining is gated by the alliance's `admission_price` (a `befriend_price`-style task: e.g. raze a named number of a rival bloc's starbases, or obey the leader) and, where set, by a `membership_gate` — a specific species whose consent is required (the leader, or a designated gatekeeper, must authorize entry; junior members cannot admit the player alone). On joining:

- every member species receives a positive **attitude offset** (their effective disposition toward the player rises), and members are bound by a **no-attack obligation** — attacking a fellow member voids membership and reverses the bonus;
- every species in each `rival_alliance_id` receives a negative attitude offset (rivals turn cold or hostile) — **signing with one bloc closes doors with its rivals**, the central diplomatic tension;
- unaligned species are unaffected except through their own inter-species relations (§6.4).

`alliance_role` distinguishes a **leader** (sets admission price, can be the gate), a **member**, and an **aspirant** (an unaligned species angling to join — a hook for emergent realignment). An optional `internal_rival_species_id` models a junior member scheming to usurp the leader; for v1 this is flavor/intel surfaced in the alien dossier, with an optional config-gated leadership-change event left to Phase 5's richer interactions. Full diplomacy trees remain out of scope; alliance membership is a single exclusive choice with scalar standing, consistent with §2's non-goals.

### 6.4 Inter-species disposition

Disposition is not only player-facing: species hold stances **toward each other**, which drives NPC-vs-NPC behavior and how befriending one party shifts your standing with others. This is a `relations` block in the roster — a sparse, possibly asymmetric matrix mapping an ordered pair `(species_a, species_b)` to a relation value on `0.0` (hatred) … `1.0` (amity), or to a modifier applied atop a derived default. Defaults are derived, overrides are explicit:

- **Derived default:** same alliance ⇒ friendly; rival alliances ⇒ hostile; otherwise neutral. Computed at big bang from the alliance graph so a roster need not spell out every pair.
- **Explicit overrides:** named affinities and hatreds that cut across alliance lines — an aesthetics-driven loathing between two otherwise-unallied species, an infatuation that explains why a species joined a bloc at all. These are the entries that give a roster character.

Inter-species relations feed three systems: (1) **NPC AI** — when ships of two species meet in a sector, their mutual relation decides whether they ignore, trade with, or fight each other (`engine.npc`); (2) **reputation spillover** — helping or harming species X nudges the player's attitude with X's friends and enemies in proportion to the relation (favor an alliance's enemy and its members cool toward you); (3) **alliance cohesion and grudges** (§6.5). The validator asserts relations stay consistent with alliance membership unless an explicit override is present.

### 6.5 Grudges and vendettas

Some stances are **durable, dated grudges** rather than standing relations — the reference roster is full of them ("a 16-orbit grudge," "a 524-orbit grudge over a named individual"). A `Grudge` (§4) records `holder`, `target`, `cause`, `severity`, `created_at`, and `duration` (finite, or infinite for `memory_model=never_forgets`), and may carry a linked **demand** (a `befriend_price` task the grudge spawns — e.g. a jealous species appends "destroy that rival's starbase" as the price of its regard, marking its members with a vendetta tag). Grudges come from two sources:

- **Seeded** — declared in the roster between species; instantiated at big bang and decaying (or not) over game-days.
- **Runtime** — generated by player conduct. Attacking a species creates a grudge against the player; against a `never_forgets` / `betrayal_model=permanent` species it never decays and floors the attitude offset, locking that species out permanently. A `recoverable` species' grudge decays at its `attitude_gain_rate` as the player makes amends.

Grudges modify the relevant relation/attitude while active and can gate or spawn demands, giving the diplomacy layer memory and momentum without a full diplomacy tree.

### 6.6 Combat threat tiers

`threat_tier` (fearsome / worthy / feeble / special) is a **narrative difficulty band kept deliberately separate from the numeric `threat_rating`**, because the reference catalogue shows the two diverge: a tiny, fragile hull can be "lethal one-on-one," and a species can be "feared out of all proportion to its stats." The tier is config and drives (1) encounter pacing — `special`/`fearsome` species are rarer and weightier (consistent with the rarity-inverse-to-threat rule, §6 disposition prose and §10); and (2) the in-game **alien dossier** (§11), where one faction can be configured to *narrate the others* — ranking every species as a foe — turning flavor text into a usable, in-world difficulty guide. `special` flags the irreducibly weird cases (non-combatants, judges, infestation-only "combat," engine-stealing weapons) that don't sort onto a linear scale.

### 6.7 Dialogue and conversation

An encounter is not only a disposition roll and a stat exchange — it is a **conversation**, and the conversation is where a species' `persona` becomes audible. The reference catalogue carries a full **per-species dialogue corpus** (`lightspeed-analysis/Lightspeed_Alien_Dialogue_Reference.md`): branching prose keyed to the player's standing, a menu of conversational verbs, and an unmistakable *voice* per species — the Venge's inverted syntax, the Lutin's literal-minded machine grammar, the Monad's biblical cadence, the Stentor's mangled-translator garble, the Dignee's asterisk-delimited concept-fragment telepathy, the Vorticella's bombastic capitalised warrior-speak. We reproduce this as **config-driven dialogue**, authored in the roster and selected at runtime, never hard-coded.

**Dialogue is data: a `dialogue_pack`.** Each species' roster entry carries (or inherits, via its `persona`) a `dialogue_pack` — a map from a **closed vocabulary of context keys** to lists of **line entries**. A context key names an interaction moment: `greeting`, `trade_open` / `trade_refuse`, `treaty_offer` / `treaty_grant` / `treaty_condition` / `treaty_refuse`, `combat_open` / `combat_taunt` / `surrender`, `flee_scorn`, `extort_response`, `refuel`, `demand`, `reward`, `betrayal`, `farewell`, the **signature-mechanic prompts** (`sig.trojan_gift.lower_shields`, `sig.reprogram_unlock.offer`, `sig.morality_judge.verdict`, …, §6.2), and the **dossier** lines `dossier_self` and `dossier_other` (the latter parameterised by the subject species — this is the in-world face of §6.4 inter-species relations and the §6.6/§11 alien bestiary, where one faction narrates the others). The player's **reply menu** on the contact screen is **authored data**: it is the active node's `choices` (numbered player replies), resolved through the same `species → persona → generic` fallback the lines use — but for choices. The `generic` persona authors the **baseline reply menu** on `start_context` (default `greeting`), and any node whose resolved line carries no `choices` of its own falls back to it (`select.choices_for`), so the menu is always config-defined and there is **no code-derived verb menu**. The baseline covers the peaceful Phase-2 interactions — ASK-ABOUT (a `dossier_other` line about a chosen known species), ASK-FOR-COORDINATES (`offer_coordinates`), TRADE, BARTER, and FAREWELL — each issuing a single conversation action that speaks its line and advances that context's recency ring (the same `greeting`-keyed mechanism the hail already uses, lifted to every peaceful context). Treaty replies are authored too but stay **gated-and-greyed** until their state machine ships (Phase 5); the **attack reply is live** (WP70) — it ends the conversation and opens first-strike combat via `AttackSpecies`, gated by the shared `encounters.first_strike_block` (Core sanctuary, the Entity, an `influence_gate` forbid, shipless kinds). A reply's **availability** is computed from the species' parameters and the live encounter (`combatant`, `trade_posture`, `treaty_mode`, artifacts on offer, a coordinate tip) — `session._gate_choice` — so a disabled reply shows *why* it is greyed, exactly as the old derived menu did. A species or persona may author its **own** replies on any node to override the baseline (e.g. a branch into a workshop), and the generic baseline carries every species that authors none.

**Selection is salience-scored (a fact dictionary against a rules database).** Following the Valve "AI-driven dynamic dialog" / salience-based-narrative pattern, each contact assembles a **fact dictionary** from encounter state — the **standing band** (effective disposition bucketed hostile / wary / neutral / friendly, plus an *allied* band when the player shares the species' alliance), whether a treaty is in force, any active **grudge** or `betrayal_model=permanent` lock, the species' current `trade_posture` / `treaty_mode`, the stage reached on its `signature_mechanic` or `befriend_price` ladder, player needs (low fuel, sought aspect), intel availability, and transient flags (player just fled, player just attacked an ally). A line entry is `{when: <criteria>, variants|grammar, weight}`; its `when` pins a subset of those facts (`standing`/`treaty` as first-class keys plus an open `criteria` map). For the active context the engine keeps every entry whose criteria all hold and lets the **most-specific match win** — the entry pinning the most facts — ties broken by `weight` through the game's seeded RNG. **Standing is the heaviest axis**: a pinned `standing` scores double (`select.STANDING_WEIGHT`), so how a species feels about the player outranks any single situational criterion — a `standing: hostile` greeting beats the same pack's "we have met before" line instead of tying with it and being settled by a coin flip. Because the chain never blends packs, a pack that authors *any* entry for a context claims it outright: a species pack with a catch-all greeting must author its own hostile opener, since `generic`'s standing-keyed lines are then unreachable for that species (the default corpus does exactly this — every species greets an enemy in its own voice). This is what lets one species greet a friend, a stranger, and an enemy in three different voices from a single `greeting` key, and lets new facts gate lines (a friendly high-tech alien volunteering coordinates) without touching the matcher — exactly the standing-keyed branching the corpus shows, generalised.

**Realisation: variant pools or LLM-authored Tracery grammars; a recency ring prevents repeats.** An entry realises its beat one of two ways. A `variants` pool is interchangeable phrasings of the same beat. A `grammar` is a compact **Tracery** rule map (expanded from `origin`, referencing shared persona/global fragments) **authored offline by a local or cloud LLM** and baked into config, so a few rules yield combinatorial variety — concepts recombined, not sentence fragments stitched. Either way the selector draws through the seeded RNG **excluding the last *K* indices shown for that `(species instance, context)` slot** (a small recency ring, `K` config, default 2, keyed **per contact instance** — two ships of one species do not share a "what I already said" ring), so a player meeting the same contact repeatedly hears the beat rephrased rather than verbatim. Grammar expansion is itself seeded (pytracery draws from the global RNG, which the renderer seeds from a derived string and restores), so **no model runs in the client** and dialogue stays **reproducible from `(seed, command log)`** like everything else, while never affecting game state — dialogue is cosmetic, reporting outcomes the rules have already decided.

**Encounter-based branching state (Phase 3): sessions, situational facts, and arcs.** The fact dictionary grows three layers so a conversation can branch on *this* encounter and remember earlier ones — all replay-safe, because every layer is core state reconstructed by the command log, never UI memory. (1) A **per-contact session** (`Player.contact_session`: the species instance, the sector, and a small fact map) opens at hail and accumulates what happened *this visit* — topics asked, offers seen, reactions given — feeding `when.criteria` so an alien can react to being asked the same thing twice, circle back to a refused offer, or harden after an insult. Its lifetime is structural, not polite: it is cleared by **farewell and by every movement or encounter reducer**, so a player who warps off mid-sentence never leaves a stale session behind (the UI is not trusted to close it). (2) **Situational facts** describe the live circumstances — distance band, sector features (a nebula, a wreck), the player's hull/fuel state buckets, cargo aboard, and `just_fled_combat` (derived from `Player.last_combat`, a small record combat reducers write) — so authored lines can name the actual situation. (3) **Persisted per-species arc flags** (`Player.species_arcs`, set by authored choice actions and signature-mechanic stages) unlock branches *across* visits, and **callback facts** derived from `species_last_seen`, logged leads, and `last_combat` let a speaker say "back again so soon?", ask whether the coordinates panned out, or sneer at the patrol you fled. With Phase 3 the **`sig.*` signature-mechanic prompts** (§6.2) also go live: a `choices` reply into a `sig.*` context runs the species' hook (`core.mechanics`), which persists its ladder stage in `species_arcs` — surfaced as the `sig_stage` fact — so the verdict/response line gates on the *persisted* stage (never a transient outcome), keeping the view/reducer lockstep intact. Fact assembly lives in one shared pure module (`edge/dialogue/facts.py`) used identically by the `Converse` reducer and the projection, preserving the view/reducer lockstep below. With Phase 3 the **combat contexts** (`combat_open`, `combat_taunt`, `surrender`, `flee_scorn`, `betrayal`) flip from authored-but-inert to live: they are spoken from the encounter reducers keyed to encounter facts (round, pack size and survivors, a bloodied pack, downed shields, fleeing) and advance the recency rings exactly as `Converse` does, while conversational access to them stays blocked.

**Authored player-chosen replies (the whole menu).** Every contact node's reply menu is its **authored `choices` list** — numbered player replies. There is **no code-derived verb fallback**: choices are resolved by `select.choices_for`, which returns the winning line's pack `choices` or, when that pack authors none, the **`generic` persona's choices for the same context** (the `start_context` baseline). So a persona may voice a greeting line without restating the menu — its replies fall through to generic — while a pack that *does* author its own replies (e.g. a branch into a workshop) overrides the baseline for that node. **The screen's only letter shortcuts are `b` (Back) and `f` (Farewell)**; both are meta-navigation independent of the menu (`f` speaks a parting line then breaks contact; `b` steps back along the breadcrumb). The play-test harness adds a dev-only **`f5` (Refresh)** that re-rolls the current context's line in place to audition its variant pool. The **farewell** reply (an authored `leave` choice) always sorts **last** in the rendered menu (a stable reorder; dispatch is by canonical index, so position is cosmetic). Hosts can override what *break contact* does via the screen's `on_exit` hook — the play-test harness points it at its controls modal so a farewell opens the dials instead of a blank screen. A `DialogueChoice` is `{text, next_context?, action?, when?, weight}`: `text` is the player's reply (templated with the node's placeholders), `next_context` transitions to another context — any known intent, a reserved **`branch.*` node** (an authored intermediate beat that exists only as a choice target, the strict-vocabulary alternative to free-form keys; `is_known_context` accepts `branch.*` like `sig.*`), or the special value `"back"` to return to the previous menu node, and `action` is one of `CHOICE_ACTIONS` (`leave` / `trade` / `barter` / `accept_lead` / `accept_contract` / `attack`; `accept_contract` books the favor the speaker offers on the `contract_offer` node — gated on a live offer (`has_contract_offer`), §14/WP57; `attack` ends the conversation and opens **first-strike combat** — the reducer delegates to `AttackSpecies`, WP70). A choice's `when` gates whether it is offered, scored like a line's `when`; beyond that, each reply's **availability** (and the reason it is greyed) is computed at projection time from the species' params and live offers (`session._gate_choice`: `trade_posture`, `treaty_mode`, attackability per `first_strike_block`, artifacts on offer, a coordinate tip), and disabled replies are hidden unless `ui.show_disabled_options`. **Conversation position is not stored in core state** — it rides on the command: `Converse(species_id, context, choice_index=…)` names the node currently shown, and the reducer **re-resolves that node's menu via the same `choices_for`** (read-only, with the same `encounter_rng` inputs the projection used, so it indexes the very choices the player saw — view and reducer stay in lockstep), checks the index and the choice's `when`, then applies the transition and/or action. So branching adds **no `Player` fields and no `state_hash`/replay churn**, and a reply stays reproducible from `(seed, command log)`. The §13 validator: the `generic` persona must author a non-empty `choices` list on `start_context` (the baseline menu); every choice names a valid `action` and a known `next_context` (skipping `"back"` targets); every `branch.*` node is reachable (targeted by some choice); and every node reachable through choices resolves a non-empty line for all standings (a `branch.*` node therefore carries a catch-all, mirroring the generic-persona invariant). The authoring pipeline's `output_schema` emits `choices` alongside the Tracery `grammar` so a backend can draft persona-voiced replies offline.

**Aliens hand out maps (the location-tip mechanic).** The `discovery` concept adds an `offer_coordinates` intent: a friendly or allied speaker can volunteer **route coordinates to a place the player has not yet reached** — a rare relic, or a forward starbase with better hardware. The big bang gives each species *kind* a seeded, band-weighted **knowledge table** of such places (`UniverseState.species_knowledge`); at contact, a pure planner (`edge.dialogue.intel.pick_intel_target`) gates by disposition, drops anything the player has explored, logged, or collected, ranks the rest by rarity × distance, and binds `{target}`/`{coords}`/`{distance}`/`{band}`/`{reward}` for the line. Accepting the tip records a **`Lead`** on the player (an `AcceptLead` command, replayable like any other), surfaced on the Computer/Map screen as a plottable route. Because a logged lead *is* the coordinates, it is the **one exception to TravelTo's charted-only routing** (§9/§11): the player may `TravelTo` a lead's destination over the **full** warp graph — the computer plots and flies the course, charting each hop as it goes — whereas every non-lead destination still requires an already-uncovered route, so exploration elsewhere is not trivialised. The UI gates accepting: the **Log-coordinates** verb is enabled only once the player has *asked* (the `offer_coordinates` line is on screen) and a tip is actually on offer — you cannot log a route the speaker has not volunteered. This activates the friendly slice of the `coordinate_broker` / `passage_broker` signature hooks (§6.2) early; the hostile broker behaviours (extort / mislead) land with Phase 3.

**Templates fill from context.** Variant strings are templates with `{placeholders}` resolved at render time from an interaction context dict — `{subject}` species or alliance name, `{count}` of systems / components, `{coords}` of a contract target, `{player}`, `{reward}` — mirroring the corpus's `%s` slots ("Destroy a {subject} world", "control {count} more stellar systems"). An empty or unresolved pool **falls back up the chain species → persona → generic**, so a sparse roster entry still speaks in its persona's voice and a missing line never blanks the screen.

**Persona packs keep authoring light.** A `persona` (§6.1) is itself a shareable `dialogue_pack` of generic, voice-correct lines (a Yoda-syntax pack, a literalist-machine pack, a biblical-judge pack); a species inherits it and overrides only the beats that make it distinctive. The validator (§13) checks that every species resolves a non-empty line — variant pool **or** grammar — for each context its parameters can reach, that every template placeholder (in variants and grammar strings alike) is fillable, and that `dossier_other` covers the species its `relations` / roster references can name.

**The dialogue system lives in `edge/dialogue/`** — a pure, core-level package (it imports the lower `edge.core` modules and is consumed by `edge.core.rules` / `edge.server`, so the graph stays acyclic and I/O-free): `intents` (the closed concept→intent vocabulary), `select` (the salience-scored selector, recency ring, validator), `render` (deterministic Tracery realisation), and `intel` (the location-tip planner + knowledge table). The shared `DialogueLine` schema, `Player.dialogue_recency`, and `Player.leads` stay in `edge.core`. The **offline authoring pipeline** is the one impure corner, isolated in `edge/dialogue/authoring/` (dev-only, never imported by runtime): a pluggable `Backend` — local **Ollama** (default), the **Anthropic API**, **Antigravity**, or an authenticated **agent CLI session** (`claude` / `agy` / a generic `cli` template, reusing a logged-in CLI so no API key is needed; the prompt/schema are exchanged through a throwaway temp dir the tool always removes) — generates per-species, per-intent Tracery grammars as schema-constrained JSON, validates and render-smoke-tests them, and writes a config sidecar named for the backend and model that produced it (`config/dialogue/alien_dialogue.<backend>_<model>.yaml`, so corpora from different models don't clobber each other). It ships as the `edge-author-dialogue` console script (`pixi run author-dialogue`). Alongside it, a dev-only **play-test harness** (`edge/dialogue/authoring/playtest.py`, the `edge-playtest-dialogue` script / `--playtest` flag) drives the **real** `AlienContactScreen` and `server.session.contact_view` against a synthetic single-game universe (one instance of every roster species), with a one-key controls modal to switch the simulated species, standing band, treaty/intel state, and a **force-enable** toggle that makes gated verbs/choices selectable so every branch can be walked — letting an author *hear* a freshly-authored sidecar (`--sidecar`) in motion. It is the **sole** place `edge.dialogue.authoring` imports `edge.tui` (an upward import permitted only because this corner is dev-only and never on the runtime path; the CLI loads it lazily). Backtracking out of a dead-end branch node (Backspace) is a property of the shared contact screen itself, so it serves the live game too.

Alien species rosters — names, archetypes, base disposition centers and variance, tech curves, threat/interception tables and threat tiers, encounter weights, barter preferences, signature mechanics, pack/escort composition, fleets and starbase policy, `persona` voice keys, the alliance blocs and their admission prices, the inter-species relation matrix, seeded grudges, and which roster a game uses — are all config data, not code. The **dialogue corpus itself** (the `personas` packs, `recency_k`, and shared `grammar` fragments — and any per-species `dialogue_pack` overrides) lives in a **separate dialogue file** (`config/alien_dialogue_default.yaml`), pointed to by `dialogue_file:` and merged onto the roster at the config I/O seam, so a roster and its voice corpus vary independently. `dialogue_file:` accepts a **single path or a list applied in order**: the base file supplies `personas`/`recency_k`/`grammar`, and any later file may carry a `species_grammars` block (an authoring-pipeline sidecar, §6.7) that the loader **splices into each species' `dialogue_pack` by id** — so a machine-authored corpus can be A/B-pointed-at directly without restating the base packs.

## 7. Exploration and Discovery

Discovery is the game's central reward system: the universe is salted at big bang with things to find, and finding them is how the player advances beyond what latinum alone can buy.

**Discovery classes.**
- *Astronomical phenomena* — nebulae (sensor interference: ships inside are harder to detect, a hiding mechanic that stacks with cloaking), black holes (navigation hazards with configurable behavior, from damage-on-approach to one-way gravity warps), and rarer singular phenomena.
- *Derelict shipwrecks* — salvageable in space for cargo, latinum, technology, and log fragments that hint at the locations of other discoveries. A ship destroyed in NPC combat also leaves one **persistent, immediately visible wreck discovery per destroyed hull** in the battle sector. Its deterministic cache is rolled at destruction from the combat-salvage configuration, but no cache value is credited automatically: the wreck remains in the sector view as evidence of the fight until a player spends the ordinary discovery-salvage turn to collect it. Collection transfers its latinum and any loose components atomically, logs the wreck in the codex, and cannot be repeated. The destroyed roaming contact is removed from the universe; player/PvP pod-and-salvage rules and razed fixed emplacements remain separate.
- *The Entity* — **one roaming, dialogue-only singular being per universe**, not a salted object. The `entity` discovery kind is **never rolled on the space tables**; instead the big bang draws the roster species flagged `singular_entity` (§6.1 — the Concordance in the default roster: a bodiless cosmic arbiter met as a voice) and fields it as exactly one instance that **drifts through the universe** on the engine tick like any alien contact (§10). It fields no ships and can never be fought — the encounter *is* the conversation (its `signature_mechanic`, e.g. the morality judge, is delivered through dialogue), and on the contact screen its artwork fills the slot where ship art would render. Finding it is a **pursuit**: friendly aliens volunteer a lead recording its **last-known sector** (which goes stale as it moves — re-ask, or sweep nearby space); in-sector, the sensor panel **always registers an anomalous presence**, but resolving that presence into contact is **gated by sensor rating** at Legendary difficulty. First contact collects a reserved hidden **Legendary codex entry**, so meeting it still counts among the game's great discoveries.
- *Planets* — beyond their classic TW2002 colony role, planets can be **descended onto**. Surface exploration reveals sites: ruins, artifacts, ancient technology caches, crashed ships. A planet's **`planet_type` and `owner` (§4.2)** shape both its production value and its discovery value — the least habitable, unowned *landable* worlds of the deep bands (barren rocks) make poor colonies but often hide the richest ruins (asteroid belts are non-landable spatial features — scanned and mined in orbit, never descended onto, §4.2), and a **derelict orbital starbase** is itself a discovery: a powered-down component cache to strip or repair.

**Discoveries are named (PT-49, WP-PR2-04).** Every discovery carries a stored `name` — a find is a *place you can talk about*, "the Cygnus Veil", not "Nebula ∗ Rare" (the kind and rarity remain, as the subtitle they always were). The name is stamped at creation from **per-kind config pools** (`names.discoveries`, keyed by `DiscoveryKind`) drawn combinatorially and **without replacement**, so no two finds in a universe share a name; an exhausted pool falls through to numbering ("Black Hole 1"). Naming draws from a **names-only sub-RNG** seeded off the game seed, never from the placement RNG — so a name is deterministic under `(seed, command log)` replay, *and* adding or editing a name pool cannot move a single discovery, protecting the rarity gradient below and every golden replay. One exception carries the rule's intent: a **combat wreck takes the destroyed ship's own name**, so the hulk drifting in the battle sector is recognisably the raider you shot down. `name` is live state (it is in `state_hash`); the projection falls back to the kind for a nameless row, so a blank never reaches the player.

**Rarity gradient.** Every discovery has a rarity tier — Common, Uncommon, Rare, Exceptional, Legendary. Two rules govern placement: (1) rarity probability shifts upward with the sector's distance band, so Core Space neighborhoods hold only common curiosities while the deep frontier holds the legendary finds; and (2) value scales with rarity, specifically *technology-progression* value — rare finds yield aspect upgrades, unique devices, and barter goods that no amount of near-home trading can buy. Band weights and tier payout tables are config data; the big bang validator asserts the gradient is monotonic.

**Discovery flow.** Entering a sector reveals its obvious features; the ship's sensor rating determines whether hidden discoveries (drifting wrecks, nebula-shrouded sites) are detected, so sensors are a real progression axis. Planet descent costs turns and presents the planet's surface sites for exploration one at a time. All outcomes draw from the game's seeded RNG: a given seed always buries the same treasures in the same places.

**The loop.** Trade near home → buy engine/shield/sensor upgrades → push one band deeper → discover → barter finds with high-tech friendly species for better upgrades → push deeper still. Trading remains the reliable income floor; discovery is the progression ceiling.

## 8. Economy

**Pricing.** BNT's parameterized linear model with terminal-space's stock-ratio shape, denominated in gold-pressed latinum, and **normative over the reference formulas in §A.2–§A.3 wherever they differ**. Let `stock_ratio = stock / capacity` with `capacity = size × 1000` (the twclone capacity model, §A.1). Per commodity `c` at a port: when the port *sells* `c` (player buys), `price(c) = base[c] − delta[c] × stock_ratio × elasticity[c]`; when it *buys* `c` (player sells), `price(c) = base[c] + delta[c] × (1 − stock_ratio) × elasticity[c]`. Both forms must give **negative feedback** — buying from a port raises its sell price, selling into a port lowers its buy price — so the loop self-limits and cannot be pumped. (NB: §A.2's reported buy-side, read literally, *inverts* this; do **not** copy it — selling into a port must always lower what it pays.) The result is **clamped** to `[price_floor[c], price_ceiling[c]]`, `price_floor = floor_frac × base` (default `floor_frac = 0.25`), so prices stay positive and bounded even when `elasticity > 1` or regen overshoots capacity — a core invariant with its own property test. Defaults (per-unit latinum): fuel ore base 11 delta 5; organics base 5 delta 2; equipment base 15 delta 7 (BNT's tuned values, relabeled to the TW trio); `elasticity[c]` default 1.0, per-commodity tunable. All constants in config.

**Economy constants (defaults; all config).** The trade loop's central balance ratio is *income-per-day ÷ cost-of-first-upgrade*, tuned so a first meaningful upgrade lands inside the opening session (target ≈ 2–4 hours' play, well under one game-day — the Phase-1 exit criterion, §14):
- **Starting capital:** 2,000 latinum on hand, 0 banked — enough to stock a starter hold (~30 units × ~10/unit) with buffer.
- **Components (the upgrade sink):** Tier I priced in latinum (default ~2,000 each) so trading alone funds the first slot-fill or base-part swap; Tier II is latinum + artifact barter (default ~8,000 latinum-equiv); Tier III is barter/discovery only (no straight latinum sale). One filled engine-room slot ≈ one Tier-I purchase ≈ a few hours' trading.
- **Barter equivalence:** a discovery's rarity tier maps to a component tier for barter — Rare artifact ≈ Tier II, Exceptional ≈ Tier III — each carrying a latinum-equivalent the contact screen shows, so the player can weigh a barter against a cash price.
- **Ship hulls:** starter (Trailblazer-class) is free; later hulls priced in config (rough band ~20k–150k latinum, rising with role), bought at Stardock.
- **Repair:** repair-kits ~200 latinum each (a light hold cost); full Stardock / friendly-base restoration of a knocked-out component ≈ 25% of that component's tier price.
- **Faucet/sink model:** latinum is **not conserved** — it is *minted* when a port buys from the player and *burned* when it sells (the §A.1 conservation invariant covers goods only). In Phase 1–4 `Port.latinum` is a soft accounting figure, not a hard constraint (ports never go broke). Under the Phase-5 order book it becomes a **real, drainable purse**: a port buying from the player can pay only what its purse holds — the trade **partially fills** to the affordable quantity and reports the shortfall honestly — while selling to the player credits the purse, daily settlement moves purses between ports (conserving, above), and the **liquidity drip** is the standing faucet that keeps every purse above a configured floor. NPC merchants trade against the same purses, so a working arbitrageur can genuinely drain a small market. Because movement costs turns, not fuel, the standing latinum **sinks** are component/ship purchases and repair; these must stay live across the whole game (Tier-I/II component buys, Stardock repair, forward-base service fees, citadel builds, tavern rumors — §4.2, §14) to absorb the interest and discovery-payload **faucets** and head off mid-game latinum inflation.

**Role of trading.** Trading is deliberately the means, not the end: profit curves are tuned so that pair-trading reliably funds early aspect upgrades (engine, shields, sensors, holds), while the highest technology tiers are gated behind alien barter and rare discoveries that latinum alone cannot buy. The pair-trade finder and route planner keep the trading loop tight so it stays a fun engine for exploration rather than a grind.

**Haggling.** TW2002's signature negotiation, implemented as a bounded mini-game: the port quotes; the player counter-offers; acceptance probability falls off with distance from fair price and with the player's recent haggling history at that port; 2 rejections end negotiation at the port's final price; an insulting offer (>~30% off fair) aborts the trade. Tunable; can be disabled for "quick trade" mode.

**Stock regeneration and the order-book market.** Phase 1–4 ships simple regen: each economy tick (hourly game time), stock moves 5% toward the port's desired level (50% capacity standard, 90% Stardock — twclone ratios). Phase 5 replaces this with the **order-book market** (twclone's model, normative here). On the hourly economy tick each port posts **at most one order per commodity per side**: a **BUY** for `desired − stock` when stock sits below `desired × (1 − order_band)`, at a limit price equal to the port's own quoted §8 price at current stock (clamped so `qty × limit` never exceeds the port's purse), and a **SELL** for the excess above `desired × (1 + order_band)` at its quoted ask; each cycle *replaces* the port's previous open orders, so the book is bounded and idempotent (the `order_band` dead zone stops churn at equilibrium). **Daily settlement** matches orders per commodity — buys sorted by limit descending, sells by ask ascending, ties broken by port id — filling `min(buy qty, sell qty, buyer purse ÷ price, seller stock, buyer capacity headroom)` at the integer midpoint price and **physically moving goods and latinum between the ports**. Settlement is RNG-free, deterministic, and **conserving**: per commodity the stock deltas sum to zero, and the latinum deltas sum to zero (a §13 invariant). Two deliberate residuals keep the closed book alive: a **hinterland drift** (the old regen at a much smaller fraction — each port's off-map hinterland producing and consuming, the external gradient the market arbitrages) and a daily **liquidity drip** that tops each purse toward a configured minimum so player selling can never deadlock. All knobs live under `economy.market` in config, including an `enabled` switch that falls back to the legacy regen byte-identically.

**Banking.** Player bank accounts at Stardock with modest interest (BNT's 1.0005/tick compounded is the reference; we tune to ~0.5%/game-day) and corp accounts. Invariants enforced in one place (`core.economy`): balances never negative, goods conserved, every mutation inside a transaction.

**Planetary production and ownership.** Colonized planets produce per BlackNova's colonist model (§A.3) — colonists generate ore / organics / equipment / fighters per tick by player-set allocation sliders, with food consumption, starvation, reproduction, and interest — but the output is **shaped by `planet_type` (§4.2)**: a type's `yield_profile` multiplies its per-commodity production (a `terrestrial_hot` world pours out Fuel Ore but little else; a `terrestrial_warm` world is an Organics engine), and its `habitability` caps colonist population and growth (watery `cool` worlds sustain the largest colonies; irradiated `hot` and frozen `cold` worlds support only sparse ones). Uncolonizable types yield through extraction rather than colonists — `jovian` fuel-scooping and `asteroid_belt` raw mining — and `barren` worlds produce nothing. Only a planet's **owner** collects its production: an alliance for its holdings, the player for worlds they have **claimed** (colonizing an unowned habitable world is the player's path to a deep-space production base, §4.2). Core worlds, owned by the governing alliance, are off-limits to player colonization. The colonists who settle a world are **recruited, not bought** — peopled from Stardock's recruitment office (a per-head latinum incentive) or by emigration from inhabited worlds friendly to the player, carried against the ship's separate occupancy limit rather than its cargo holds (§4.2). They are beings with a choice, never a tradeable commodity.

**NPC traders.** Friendly-disposition merchant ships are Ferengi-style mobile arbitrageurs (twclone model): they roam nearby sectors, compare port prices, execute real trades that move real stock and latinum, and hold persistent cargo/cash under their species' corporation. They are the economy's circulation system, and trading alongside them builds attitude with that species.

## 9. Time, Turns, and the Engine

Per the original TWINSTR.DOC rules: players get N turns per game-day (default 250, config); each warp move costs turns per the ship's `turns_per_warp`; docking costs 1. The engine (`engine.ticker`, an asyncio task) implements twclone's two-level scheduling: a short tick (default 1 s real time) that consumes the event log, steps NPCs in bounded batches, and runs sweepers; plus durable cron tasks — `daily_turn_reset`, `hourly_port_economy`, `planet_growth`, `interest_accrual` — with persisted `next_due_at` so a reloaded save never double-runs or skips a tick. In single-player, game time can be configured to advance only while playing, or in real time.

## 10. Encounters, Combat, and Territory

**Alien encounters.** Moving through or lingering in inhabited territory rolls for encounters from the region's alien species table, with weights inverse to threat rating (Section 6): common raiders harass the Frontier; the apex species of the Void are rarely seen and never forgotten. The encounter is spawned as a **group** per the species' `pack_behavior`/`escort` (§6) — a lone hunter, a warship shadowed by a screen of fighters, a swarm in numbers, a colonial cluster — so the same `threat_rating` can present very differently. Whether a given encounter opens peacefully or with violence is then rolled against the species' effective disposition (Section 6), further shifted by any active **grudge** the species holds against the player and by **alliance standing** (a fellow-member greets warmly; a rival-bloc member is primed to attack) — a high-disposition species greets the player and offers trade, a low-disposition one attacks on sight, and the wary middle is a genuine coin-flip. The greeting, combat taunts, surrender, and flee-scorn lines spoken throughout are drawn from the species' **`dialogue_pack`** keyed to the resolved standing band (§6.7), so the same encounter outcome reads in that species' own voice and rarely repeats verbatim. A `combatant=false` species can never reach violence; its "threat" is delivered through diplomacy (a `signature_mechanic`, §6.2) instead. A hostile-turning encounter opens with a detection check — the species' sensors against the player's cloak rating plus any nebula cover; an undetected player may slip away freely, making stealth a genuine alternative to firepower. The player can also strike first (WP70): the **`AttackSpecies`** command — issued from the sector view or as an authored contact-screen `attack` reply — spawns the species' pack through the same machinery, and the §6.5 souring rail fires at initiation (a first strike is remembered like one kill, with an honest grudge cause), so betrayal costs even a player who then flees. Its gates live in the pure `encounters.first_strike_block`, shared with the contact projection: no strikes in the Core sanctuary, none on the Entity or from/at an escape pod, an `influence_gate` species may forbid it, and a shipless kind offers nothing to fight. Likewise **`AttackPlayer`** (§14, WP67) opens PvP from the same sector-view affordance — other players project into `SectorDTO.ships` with corp tag and outlaw marker (WP70). Once engaged, each round the player chooses **fight** or **flee**. Weapon **firing arcs** (§4) shape the fight: an `ahead`/`spinal` attacker is evaded by maneuvering out of its firing line (the classic counter to a fast hull with a deadly forward gun), while `all_round` armament leaves no safe angle; weapon `special`s (engine-flux, proton-stalker pods) impose their own counters. The player's own offense is the spinal **Main Gun** (its damage and rate of fire derived from that subsystem's surviving components, §4.1) plus finite **homing missiles** that ignore arc — the answer to a hull you cannot line up on. Flee success is a function of base chance, the player's **combat speed** (the Thrusters aspect) minus the species' interception rating, cloak rating, and accumulated hull damage — and is *clamped to a configured floor (default 10%)*, so escape is always possible even in a crippled ship; this floor is a core invariant with its own property test. Across the whole fight the Spindrive's **efficiency rating** applies one global bonus to screen deflection, combat speed, and gun damage (§4.1).

**Encounter state and commands (the replay contract).** A live encounter is **hashed core state**: a frozen `Encounter` on `Player.active_encounter` records the spawned pack (species instance ids), the round counter, and the detection/opening outcome. The interrupt roll, the detection check, the greeting-vs-violence roll, and every combat round draw from the game's command-stream RNG **inside reducers only** — projections never draw — and each round is a logged command (`CombatAction: fight / flee / launch_missile / field_patch`), so a fight replays exactly like a trade. Movement and docking commands are rejected while an encounter is live; a multi-hop `TravelTo` halts at the interrupted hop within the one command. A peaceful opening hands off to the contact screen (the §6.7 conversation, with its per-contact session); a violent one opens the encounter screen, whose spoken lines (`combat_open`, taunts, surrender, flee-scorn) are the species pack's combat contexts keyed to encounter facts (§6.7). The **Entity** (§7) never enters this machinery's violent path — `combatant=false` short-circuits before any roll — but its **in-sector presence hint** rides the same sector projection that announces other contacts: an anomalous reading always shown, contact gated by sensors.

**Component damage and repair (§4.1).** Shields (the Screens subsystem) absorb damage before hull. Damage that defeats the screens reduces **hull** and may additionally **knock out one subsystem component** (a roll weighted toward exposed/forward systems), immediately degrading that aspect for the rest of the fight — the gun slows, the thrusters fail and the flee floor is all that is left, or the screens themselves weaken. Repair is hybrid: a carried **repair-kit** field-patches one knocked-out component back to minimal function between rounds or sectors; full restoration, swaps, and tier upgrades wait for Stardock or a friendly alien base. Ship destruction (hull at zero) drops the player to an escape pod if owned.

**Classic stack (Phase 3).** Sector fighters: deployable in offensive/defensive/toll modes; entering a hostile-fighter sector forces engagement or retreat (retreat costs one fighter — the original rule). Mines damage on entry with deflector mitigation; in Phase 5 (implemented, WP56) the stack splits into **Armid mines** — damaging a hostile entrant on arrival (shields absorb first; a carried **mine-deflector** device absorbs hits one-for-one) — and **limpet mines**, which *attach* to the entrant's hull and mark it: while limpeted, the owner's hunting ships read the target's **exact current sector** rather than its last-known one, and removal is a paid service at any service point (§4.2). Ship-to-ship combat resolves in rounds: attacker commits fighters, hit ratios derive from ship-type odds multipliers and percentile rolls (BNT's shape), and shields absorb first. Destroyed NPC ships leave the persistent wreck discoveries specified in §7; the configured 10–20% salvage value is rolled into those caches rather than paid immediately. **NPC packs can break off (WP-PR03):** once a surviving pack's aggregate hull falls to `combat.npc_retreat_hull_frac`, a pack whose `threat_tier` allows it (fearsome/special never run) rolls `combat.npc_retreat_chance` per round to retreat — warping the surviving species instance to a legal adjacent sector (never through an interdictor, never breaking the alliance/Core occupancy rules) and ending the fight `retreated` rather than `victory`. A round that both kills some members and sees the rest flee is a mixed result: its wrecks are left and the end-of-fight copy states the exact counts ("2 destroyed, 1 broke off"). Reserve "victory" for *all* enemies destroyed. If no legal warp exists the flight fails and combat continues. Defensive devices: emergency warp (random-sector escape on trigger); cloak doubles as the pre-engagement stealth stat above. **NPC entrants trigger the same sector-entry defenses (WP-PR02):** an alien drifting on the cron into a mined/garrisoned sector whose owner opposes it (a rival bloc to an alliance-owned force, or a hostile-band disposition toward a player-owned force) has its entry resolved deterministically by the same `config.territory` formulas as a player warp — armid mines detonate, then any fighter garrison fights to a conclusion (the entrant has no interactive retreat, so either it is destroyed or the garrison is wiped). A downed NPC never reaches the destination; limpets do not attach to NPCs (deferred with corp/NPC tracking tags).

**Probes and the interdictor (Phase 5 — implemented, WP56).** A **probe** is a consumable Stardock device launched toward a target sector: it flies a shortest path over the full warp graph up to a configured hop range, charting each traversed sector into the player's explored map and reporting contents (port class, planets, contacts) to the event log — the §11 "first-class queries" promise extended to reconnaissance. Every hop through a sector held by hostile forces rolls a chance the probe is destroyed mid-flight, so deep probing is lossy. The **interdictor** is a toggleable ship device that pins the player's current sector while active — NPC ships cannot drift out of it and encounter foes cannot disengage — at a daily turn tax, so running it is a stance, not a default.

**Territory and law.** The Core Space is combat-free and deployment-free, hosts only friendly-band members of its governing alliance, and that alliance's forces punish criminal alignment there — *for those in good standing with the governor*. The Core's sanctuary follows whoever currently governs it (`Game.core_governing_alliance_id`, §6.3), not the Federation by name: a player who has joined an alliance the governor counts as a rival is treated as an enemy on entry, and the governor's forces engage them on sight, denying the home banking/trade/resupply haven until that allegiance is given up and amends are made. At game start the governor is the Federation and the player is one of its members, so the Core is home; should governance change hands (§6.3), this same rule re-evaluates against the new governor. Hostile-disposition species defend their home regions and occasionally raid trade lanes near the band boundary; raiding *their* homeworlds for legendary-tier technology caches is the single-player long game, with bounties per hostile fighter destroyed echoing the Cabal's 100/kill. Attacking any species' ships drives its attitude offset down — souring effective disposition and locking away tech offers — so piracy has a price even against peoples who greeted you warmly; against a `betrayal_model=permanent` species a single betrayal floors that offset for good (§6.5).

**Alien contact placement and drift.** A drawn species is fielded not as a lone contact but as a **cluster of ships around a home sector** — a home contact in its band plus `ships_per_home - 1` satellites scattered across non-Core sectors within `home_cluster_radius` warp-hops (config) — with a *guaranteed* contact in every live band so resupply is always reachable (§5). The Core, meanwhile, **bustles with governing-alliance traffic**: beyond the distinct members settled in their Core capital, `core_traffic` extra governing-member ships are scattered across Core sectors so the player's home region feels alive. All ships of one species share that species' standing (below), so density never fragments reputation. The Core itself stays free of placed contacts save the **Stardock hub**: a standing handful of **Core-welcome** species — the governing alliance's own members *plus unaligned neutrals* — greet every new player at the one waypoint every game funnels through (the dock sector is reserved from band/cluster placement so a rival-bloc ship can never wander into it). (The exception is deliberate: the default roster names no species as Federation members, so without seeding the hub from the broader Core-welcome set a player who starts in the Core might never meet a friendly alien; the validator exempts the dock sector from the otherwise-strict no-contacts-in-Core rule.) Contacts are **not frozen in place**: on the engine tick clock (§9) each ship rolls a configurable chance to **drift** to an adjacent sector it is *allowed* to occupy — never the Core, never a rival alliance's holding — so a species met once may have moved on by the next visit. A contact therefore appears in the live sector view only where it *currently* is, while the Computer's alien dossier records the **sector where it was last encountered** (stamped at hail), making a wandering ally something to track rather than assume. (Goal-directed movement — seeking, fleeing, patrolling — is Phase 3; this is an unbiased random walk within legal territory.)

**Sector beacons.** The player carries a **finite supply of beacons** (a counted entry in the Ship's `devices`, §4; bought at Stardock — baseline 100 latinum, §A) and can **deploy one to a sector they control** to inscribe its `beacon_text` (§4) — the short message every later visitor reads on entry, shown on the §11 sector view's `Beacons` line. A sector counts as the player's to mark when they hold it: a planet or operational orbital base they **own** there (§4.2), or — in the classic stack (Phase 3) — their own deployed **sector fighters**. Deploying **consumes one beacon** from inventory; a sector holds **at most one** beacon text, so deploying into a sector that already carries one **replaces** the existing message (no matter whose it was) rather than stacking — there is no separate "remove beacon" action, you overwrite. Consistent with the deployment-free Core (above), beacons cannot be placed in Core Space. *(Phase 3, with the territory stack.)*

**Planetary-system defense.** Many starbases hang in **orbit around a planet** (§4.2). An **operational** orbital base joins any encounter in its sector on the side of the planet's **owner**, against entrants it reads as hostile — hostility resolved through ownership: an **alliance**-owned base fights rival-bloc and at-war ships (this is how Core-governor bases enforce the Core sanctuary rule above), a **player**-owned base fights species hostile to the player, and an **unowned-but-inhabited** world defends on the inhabiting species' effective disposition. A base's defensive strength scales with its surviving components and `fusion_reactor` efficiency, so crippling the reactor or screens before an assault matters (Lightspeed's "escorts and screens first" doctrine). A **derelict** base — one whose components the big bang stripped or damaged at an unowned world (§4.2) — does not fight: with no working reactor it is just a **component-salvage cache** the player can strip (the engine-room cannibalize action, §4.1), or, owning the planet, **repair** (refill its slots) and **claim** into an operational forward foothold. Razing an owner's orbital base strips that world's defense and can flip it toward unowned and claimable.

**Starbases and alliance warfare.** A species' or alliance's starbase (§4, §4.2) is a `role=starbase` hull — immobile, shielded, armed — placed per its `starbase_policy` (a defended homeworld, scattered secret bases, an orbital planetary base, or none for nomads). Assaulting one is a set-piece combat against fixed defenses, and it is the coin of diplomacy: most alliance `admission_price`s and `contract_kill` mechanics are paid in razed rival starbases (§6.2, §6.3). Razing a base therefore raises standing with the patron that demanded it while driving the target species — and its alliance and friends (§6.4) — toward permanent hostility. **Joining an alliance** (§6.3) is itself a territory commitment: members' sectors — above all each bloc's **home cluster** just outside the Core (§5) — become safe to transit while rival-bloc clusters and space turn hostile, and breaking a member no-attack obligation voids the membership. The neutral lanes between clusters stay passable regardless of allegiance, so the outer bands are always reachable; what alignment changes is whether the bloc clusters flanking those lanes greet the player or fire on them. This is the spine of the single-player endgame — every "befriend X / raze Y" choice reshapes which regions are open and which are now hunting you.

## 11. Textual UI

**Navigation clarity.** The selected warp detail names its Coreward/outward/cross-band direction, explored state, one-way and backtrack state, immediate turn cost, avoid mark, and known target hazards with glyphs plus text. Route engagement confirmation summarizes destination, hops, turns, the honored avoid list, known hazards, and encounter interruption risk from the plotted `RouteDTO`; the TUI does not duplicate route or hazard rules.

**Unified trade presentation.** Plain ports, Stardock, and operational bases host the same `TradePanel`. It says “Port buys” / “Port sells” rather than exposing reducer enum names, aligns exact stock/capacity, unit price, carried quantity and player action, and gives the selected commodity an estimated quick-trade total, cargo-hold impact, and hard-purse limitation. Compact layouts retain commodity, port posture and action columns while moving the numeric fields into that selected-row detail. Logical commodity selection survives live market refreshes and breakpoint changes.

**Service hubs.** Stardock and orbital bases use one `ServiceHub` navigation pattern: standard/wide layouts show synchronized tabs, while compact replaces the tab rail with a scrollable keyboard/mouse selector. Services remain listed when unavailable and open an explanation of the standing, ownership, operational, or facility prerequisite; those explanations are advisory only, and command reducers remain the eligibility authority.

Textual gives us screens, CSS layout, widgets, mouse support, and free web deployment via `textual serve`. The design honors TW2002 muscle memory while exploiting modern widgets.

**Screen map.** `MainMenu` → `Game` (the primary screen) with modal/pushed screens: `PortScreen`, `PlanetScreen` (orbit view → `SurfaceScreen` for descent and site-by-site exploration), `StardockScreen` (commodities/shipyard/hardware/bank/tavern tabs — **trading is the default Commodities tab**, reusing the same `TradePanel` as `PortScreen`, so the `P` dock key or a click on a port reaches one trade UI whether or not the port is a Stardock; a plain commodities port opens `PortScreen` directly), `AlienContactScreen` (greeting, tech offers, latinum purchase and artifact barter, treaty/alliance negotiation and admission-price tasks — rendering the spoken **dialogue** above the node's **numbered authored player replies** (§6.7; the menu falls back to the generic persona's `start_context` baseline) — and a dossier panel where the contact narrates other species), `EncounterScreen` (greeting or fight/flee rounds, depending on the species' effective disposition — combat taunts/surrender lines drawn from the same dialogue pack), `EngineRoomScreen` (a top-down view of the four subsystems and their slots, §4.1 — showing knocked-out vs. healthy components, field-patch and component-swap actions, and the derived aspect each subsystem yields), and `ComputerScreen`. The old standalone Map and Messages screens are retired: direct `M`/`G` entry points open the Computer's Map/Log subviews.

**Game screen layout.** Three regions. Left 2/3: the sector view — region name, sector number, ANSI-art flavor header, contents (ports, planets, ships, fighters, beacons) as a Rich-renderable log — where a present **alien contact shows as a clickable hull token** that opens contact (a click hails *that* species; a hotkey hails the first) — laid over a **dim ASCII scene background** (a `SectorScene` widget on a lower layer that composites type-keyed sprites — sized planet > port > ship — anchored to the right edge, with the text kept to the left so art and text never share a cell; sprites are a config-style asset library, UI_MOCKUPS §1), and — as the **sole warp affordance** — a compact always-visible **nav rose** (`NavRose`): a bearing-placed compass with the player (`@`) centred, each outbound warp in the octant of its real `bearing` (§5.1 embedding; unexplored ones dimmed with `?`), a fixed `Core` anchor on the frame edge in the `core_bearing` direction for global orientation, band-tinted labels, and a recent-route breadcrumb (walked statelessly back through `entered_from`). It is baked server-side (`session.game_view` → `navstrip.build_nav_strip`, reusing the shared `canvas.Canvas` and the `MapNodeDTO` clickable-box contract) and both **click- and keyboard-navigable** — one click or Enter on the selected octant = one warp — replacing the earlier flat gravity-arrow warp list. Right 1/3: status sidebar — ship name/type, aspect readout (shields, warp speed, combat speed, cloak, sensors) with a compact subsystem-integrity line (any knocked-out components flagged, §4.1), holds bar (per-commodity fill), Main Gun / missile ammo / repair-kits, latinum, turns remaining, current distance band (the earlier sidebar region mini-map is now realised as the main-screen nav rose above). Bottom: a one-line command input plus a scrolling event ticker. **Every scene click hotspot has a focused keyboard/list equivalent**: `I` opens a right-docked **status drawer** carrying the sidebar's full readout plus a focusable *Objects here* list — planet, port/base, each ship, the Entity hint, discoveries — whose rows post the identical `Picked` routing as the scene's hotspots, so mouse and keyboard reach exactly the same interactions. The sector view is tier-responsive (WP-UI12): **compact** replaces the art scene with the location header plus that same object list inline and drops the sidebar entirely (the `I` drawer stands in for it), keeping location, objects, navigation (the nav rose stays the anchor), turns, hazards, and the latest event; **standard** keeps the grouped sidebar; **wide** enriches the sidebar with anomaly-kind detail and the Captain's-objectives checklist. Crossing a breakpoint recomposes the screen from fresh state.

**Command grammar.** Single-keystroke commands matching TW2002 where it matters — Enter/click warps to the arrow-selected nav-rose target, `P` docks at a port, `S` surveys a planet (the orbit view, from which `D` descends), `C` opens the Computer, `E` the engine room, `T` corporations, `M` the Computer's Map, `G` its Log, `I` ship status, and `^Q` quits — implemented as Textual key bindings with Esc-cancelable prompts. There is no command-line input. Every keystroke action also has a clickable/focusable affordance, and the canonical action list is available through `.`, `?`, and `Ctrl+P`.

**Computer screen** groups its fourteen query surfaces into five categories: **Navigation** (Map, Route), **Commerce** (Ports, Trade, Market), **Exploration** (Planets, Codex, Leads), **Relations** (Contracts, Alliances, Dossier, **Corp**), and **Logbook** (Log, Notes). The player's **corporation** (§4/WP66) is a relationship like a contract or an alliance, so it is a Relations subview rather than a screen behind a game-screen hotkey: its roster / treasury-and-holdings / diplomacy panels stay button-first, with the subview's pane-owned keys as accelerators for those buttons. A category remembers its last subview; compact mode uses a popup category selector, while standard/wide use category and subview rails. Direct hotkeys and links address stable subview ids. Tables share aligned/sortable columns, `/` filtering, stable-key cursor preservation, compact selected-row details, and wide side details. These are first-class engine queries rather than screen-scrapers.

**Tabbed-screen keyboard model.** On a tabbed screen, *a tab owns its action keys*. The screen binds only what is screen-wide (Back, and the category accelerators — the underlined letter in each tab title); every per-tab verb is bound onto that tab's pane, and a category pane likewise owns the **numbers** `1`..`N` addressing its own sub-tabs (each number leads its sub-tab's title). Those bindings enter the binding chain only while focus is inside the pane. The footer and the canonical action list (`.`, `?`, `Ctrl+P`) therefore advertise exactly the visible tab's verbs — never another tab's, and never a navigation key — and one key is free to carry different verbs on different tabs. **Back leads the footer on every screen**, rather than falling in behind whatever the focused widget owns. Reaching a tab — by accelerator, by number, by Enter on the tab rail, by a deep link, or by opening the screen — lands focus on that tab's primary control; a tab accelerator may not reuse a key that a focused table already owns. Two focus rules make the scoping safe. **A tab never opens focused on a text field:** a focused `Input` takes every letter as typing, which stops the tab's verbs firing, drops them from the footer, and swallows even the accelerators that would let you leave — so a filter box or amount field is auto-focused only when a tab explicitly nominates it. **A tab with no focusable content holds focus itself** (the Stardock's Bank is pure text), because a pane whose keys follow focus cannot fire keys it can never receive. Keys keep one meaning across screens: `W` routes to a typed sector on the Computer exactly as it does on the sector view, and `G` haggles at a Port exactly as it does on the Stardock's Commodities tab. The Computer (categories + numbered sub-tabs) and the Stardock/Base services hub (a flat tab row, letters only) share this model. Where a tab is **gated shut** — the Base withholds services by standing and service integrity — it carries **no keys at all**, and a single verb the current state cannot honour (assaulting or claiming a base you already hold) is likewise dropped from its tab. The footer therefore physically cannot advertise an action the reducers would refuse: availability is expressed by the absence of the keys, not by a per-action predicate.

**Aesthetics & themes.** Three registered Textual themes keep the TW2002 ANSI identity while meeting accessibility floors: `edge-ansi` (the default — cyan/yellow/magenta on black, CP437-flavored box drawing), `edge-high-contrast`, and `edge-monochrome`. Themes carry semantic tokens (focus, selection, muted, disabled, success/warning/danger) rather than screens hard-coding raw colors; body text must meet 4.5:1 contrast and focus/control indicators 3:1 against background, surface, and panel colors. A numerical WCAG relative-luminance test gates every supported theme/token/background pair at its applicable floor. Ordinary Port and orbital Starbase screens mirror Stardock's paired-art header: a species-archetype-specific **procedural cell-art icon** (palette plus `PORT_GRAMMAR` silhouette derived from that archetype's generated exterior reference) beside a matching per-service **Chafa raster banner**; standard/wide crops and compact hiding follow the Stardock seam. The sector scene and starbase-assault set piece receive that same stored archetype id and render the same procedural grammar/palette, so docking never changes a structure's visual identity. Derelict banners/icons darken and scar, while hostile ones carry alarm illumination, without changing the stored archetype. Every color-carried state also has a text or glyph representation, so `edge-monochrome` loses nothing. Optional CRT-ish flourishes (subtle starfield animation on the title screen — terminal-space's terminaltexteffects idea, reimplemented with Textual animation primitives) are disabled by the reduced-motion preference or the `--plain` flag.

**Responsive layout tiers.** One central `layout_tier(width, height)` classifies the terminal: **compact** (80–99 cols or 24–29 rows — gameplay information wins over art), **standard** (100–119 × ≥30; screens are authored at 100×34), **wide** (≥120×36 — richer art and detail panes), and **unsupported** below 80×24, where a size notice overlays the app while keeping Help (`?`) and Quit (`^Q`) live and popping automatically once the terminal regrows. The tier is applied as a CSS class across the whole screen stack on every resize; crossing a breakpoint restyles in place and must never drop focus, selection, scroll position, or in-progress input. Under space pressure, decorative art is removed before gameplay information.

**Action discovery.** Each screen advertises one canonical `ActionDescriptor` list (id, title, help, key, enabled state + prerequisite reason, danger level) derived from its key bindings; the footer, the `.` numbered action menu, the `?` contextual help, Textual's `Ctrl+P` command palette, and click affordances all render from that single list, so they can never disagree (parity and key-collision are test-enforced). Disabled actions are shown with the prerequisite that unlocks them; destructive actions always confirm, with focus defaulting to the deny option — a screen declares them in an `ACTION_DANGER` map (action → `caution`/`destructive`), the `.` menu badges them with a `!` glyph plus the word "confirms" (readable without color), and a static test proves every declared action's method routes through the shared `ConfirmScreen`. `.`, `?`, and `^Q` are reserved global keys a screen may re-surface but never repurpose.

**Feedback, focus & forms.** Toast feedback uses four standard helpers — success ("Done", 3 s), info (3 s), warning ("Warning", 4 s), error ("Error", 6 s) — so severity is carried by a title word as well as color and never hand-tuned per call site. All one-field prompts (amounts, notices, beacons, travel destination, corporation name) are the shared `FieldPrompt`: invalid input never silently dismisses — the form stays open with the typed value intact and the reason inline under the field; only a valid submit (or Esc → cancel) closes it. Dialog boxes share one `.modal-box` sizing rule with tier-scoped widths, clamped to the terminal, and scroll their bodies rather than clip, so no modal exceeds 80×24. Submits are single-shot while an action is running (the hosted lobby disables its buttons mid-join), and closing any modal returns focus to the widget that opened it.

**Presentation preferences.** Local-only `UISettings` (theme, reduced motion, art detail, density, onboarding, disabled-reply visibility) persist as JSON beside the save slot, load before the first screen mounts, save atomically, and recover from a corrupt file with defaults plus one warning. They are deliberately outside game config, universe state, replay logs, and state hashes: changing contrast or animation never alters a replay or a hosted game.

**Menu & onboarding.** The main menu leads with the likely intent — Continue (with lightweight save metadata: day, command count, last played, seed — read from the save file without replaying it) when a save exists, otherwise New game — as the sole primary action. A dismissible **Captain's objectives** strip on the game screen walks a new captain through the first-session loop (dock → trade → inspect ship → scan → discover), ticking off each act at the UI seam where it happens; progress and dismissal are local presentation state only (`UISettings.objectives_done` / `show_onboarding`, re-enabled from Options), so onboarding never touches universe state or replays and works identically on a continued game.

**Shared component workbench.** The ship Engine Room and a starbase's Station tab instantiate the **same `ComponentWorkbench` widget** (§4.1), parameterized by a presentation profile — canonical DTO subsystem names stay fixed while player-facing labels, art, colors, and slot glyphs differ per host (ship: rounded panels, `+ / ! / blank / ✓`; base: heavy industrial borders, square installation glyphs), so the two remain distinguishable even in monochrome. The widget owns slot/inventory rendering, selection, and typed selection messages; it never imports the service or command reducers — each host translates selections into its existing commands.

## 12. Persistence

SQLite, one file per game (`~/.edge/games/<name>.db`), WAL mode. Tables mirror Section 4 entities plus `event_log` and `config`. State is reconstructed as `generate(seed) + replay`, so the durable rails are the `command_log` and — because the engine's cron effects (§9) are not commands — a parallel `maintenance_log` recording each cron firing as a `(cron_name, tick, after_command_seq)` row; `rebuild` merges the two into one total order (a tick replays after the command whose `seq` it followed), re-running the pure cron reducer so no derived state is stored and `state_hash` stays a function of the log. The ticker's schedule (the `tick` counter and each cron's `next_due`) is persisted (`engine_state` / `cron_schedule`) and restored on load, delivering the §9 promise that a reloaded save never double-runs or skips a tick. Saves are implicit (every command is durable once its transaction commits — the BBS property that you can hang up mid-session and resume, per TWINSTR.DOC). `snapshots.py` adds export/import of a portable save (gzipped JSON of meta + command log + maintenance log). The repository interface is the swap point for PostgreSQL if hosted multiplayer ever demands it (twclone's lesson, pre-paid architecturally rather than adopted prematurely).

## 13. Testing Strategy

The pure core makes this cheap: property-based tests (hypothesis) for economy invariants (no negative balances, goods conservation under arbitrary trade sequences, price monotonicity in stock, prices clamped within `[floor, ceiling]` and never ≤ 0, and the negative-feedback rule that buying from a port never lowers — and selling into it never raises — that port's quoted price) and for encounter invariants (flee probability never below the configured floor under arbitrary damage/engine/interception combinations; hostile encounter weight inverse to threat rating; and dialogue selection never repeating a variant within the `K`-deep recency ring when the pool has more than `K` entries, §6.7); **dialogue-session determinism** (the projection and the `Converse` reducer resolve identical menus and lines under arbitrary session/situational/arc facts — the view/reducer lockstep of §6.7); golden-master tests replaying recorded command logs against fixed seeds and asserting final state hashes (including interrupted-`TravelTo` encounters, full fights, and branching conversations); bigbang validation tests across 100 seeds **parametrized over both `topology_mode`s** (connectivity, port-pair reachability, degree caps, in `expansive` mode no inter-region warp being a cut edge (a bridgeless lattice, so every group keeps ≥2 edge-disjoint paths to the Core), **exactly one `singular_entity` instance, never in the Core**, the Hub band hosting only friendly-band species while the Core and its neutral home lanes admit only the initial governing alliance (no rival bloc adjacent to the Core), each non-governing alliance owning exactly one home cluster smaller than the Core and not adjacent to it, at least one all-neutral path from the Core to every outer band, mean alien disposition non-increasing across distance bands, discovery rarity and value monotonically increasing across distance bands, all Core planets owned by the initial governor, the unowned-planet fraction non-decreasing across bands, any derelict starbase (stripped/damaged components) sitting on an unowned planet while every starbase on an owned planet has intact components, a habitable world reachable within the Hub, roster integrity — every `alliance_id`/`membership_gate`/`fleet`/`signature_mechanic` reference resolves, the inter-species relation matrix is consistent with alliance membership except where explicitly overridden, each alliance `admission_price` names valid rival targets, and **dialogue integrity** — every species resolves a non-empty line pool (via species → `persona` → generic fallback) for each context key its parameters can reach, every template placeholder is fillable, and `dossier_other` covers every species the roster can name (§6.7)); **Phase-5 property tests** for the market (order-book settlement conserves goods and latinum exactly — per-commodity stock deltas and inter-port latinum deltas each sum to zero; port purses never go negative and the liquidity floor keeps player selling live everywhere; order generation is RNG-free, deterministic, and idempotent — §8), governance (a flip is one atomic reduction — no replay can observe a half-flipped Core; Core planets/bases re-key and evicted species land on legal ground deterministically, while law, occupancy, and defense re-derive from the live governor with no re-keying of their own — §6.3), planetary combat (the raze-base → silence-gun → ground-assault ladder cannot be taken out of order; fighters and colonists are conserved or destroyed, never minted; Core worlds are never invadable — §4.2), and contracts (offer, progress, and completion reconstruct exactly under replay — §14); and Textual's `Pilot` test harness for UI flows (dock → haggle → buy → warp; descend → explore site → log discovery). NPC AI is tested by running headless bot-vs-engine simulations — twclone's `ai_player` bug-report harness shows how productive bot-driven QA is for this genre, and our service API is bot-friendly by construction.

### A.1 [twclone](https://github.com/rdearman/twclone) (rdearman/twclone) — C, PostgreSQL, JSON protocol

The most architecturally mature clone, and our primary structural reference. Key findings from `src/`, `docs/ENGINE.md`, `docs/GALACTIC_ECONOMY.md`, and `docs/PROTOCOL.v3/`:

**Process separation.** twclone splits into a player-facing *server* (sessions, command validation, fast reads, event emission) and a forked *engine* process (clocks, cron jobs, economy ticks, NPC stepping, FedSpace enforcement). The database is the source of truth; a TCP server-to-server channel exists only to reduce latency. The engine runs a short tick loop (250–1000 ms) consuming events in ID order, plus durable cron tasks such as `daily_turn_reset` scheduled at `daily@03:00Z`. This event-rail + cron-task model is directly portable to a Python asyncio design.

**Market-driven economy.** Rather than static stock drift, each port has `max_capacity = size * 1000` and a `desired_stock` ratio (90% for Stardock, 50% for standard ports). An hourly economy tick generates BUY orders for shortages and SELL orders for surpluses; a daily settlement matches orders where `buyer_price >= seller_price` and physically moves goods and credits between ports. Ferengi NPCs do not use the order book — they roam, compare local prices, and execute instant trades against port inventory exactly like players, with persistent cargo and bank balances. NPC factions are modeled as ordinary corporations ("Ferengi Alliance", tag FENG) with corporate bank accounts.

**Economy invariants** stated explicitly in their docs and enforced atomically: no negative bank balances, conservation of goods (trade only moves goods, production/consumption is separate planet logic), and all trades inside transactions to prevent duping. We adopt all three.

**Universe topology.** `bigbang_pg_main.c` generates sectors, reserves sectors 1–10 as protected FedSpace, generates a Stardock, and then adds *tunnels* — isolated chains of 4+ sectors (default: minimum 15 tunnels of minimum length 4, randomized up to +5) grafted onto the main graph, with NPC homeworlds placed at tunnel endpoints, followed by an `ensure_fedspace_exit` pass. Tunnels are the classic TW2002 strategic terrain (one way in, defensible).

**Scaling lesson.** twclone began on SQLite and migrated to PostgreSQL because SQLite's single-writer model bottlenecked at high concurrent player counts. For our single-host scope, SQLite is fine — but we isolate persistence behind a repository interface so the same migration is possible without touching game logic.

**Protocol catalog.** Their PROTOCOL.v3 docs enumerate the full command surface of a complete TW game: ship, player, sector/movement, trade/port, combat/weapons, planets/citadels, corporations/stock market, banking/ledger, NPC/Ferengi AI, tavern/noticeboard, and sysop commands. We use this catalog as our feature checklist and phase planning input.

### A.2 [terminal-space](https://github.com/mrdon/terminal-space) (mrdon/terminal-space) — Python, the closest cousin

A ~3,900-line Python implementation of TW2002 basics with a client/server split. This is our primary *code-level* reference because its domain model is clean, idiomatic Python:

**Domain model.** Plain classes `Galaxy`, `Sector`, `Port`, `TradingCommodity`, `Planet`, `Player`, `Ship`, `Battle`, `DroneStack`, with `to_public()` projection methods that convert internal state to client-safe Pydantic DTOs filtered through a `SessionContext`. This internal-model/public-DTO split is a pattern we adopt wholesale — it keeps fog-of-war (what the player has actually seen) enforced at the serialization boundary.

**Port classes as an enum.** The eight TW2002 port types are encoded as an enum of buy/sell triples over (Fuel Ore, Organics, Equipment): BBS=1, BSB=2, SBB=3, SSB=4, SBS=5, BSS=6, SSS=7, BBB=8. Port type distribution in their builder: types 1–3 at 20% each, types 4–6 at 10% each, type 7 at 5%, type 8 at 5%. Initial stock per commodity: `randint(200, 2000)`.

**Pricing formula.** Base costs per unit: fuel ore sells at 1 / buys at 1.5; organics 2 / 3; equipment 4 / 6. Live price scales linearly with stock ratio: when a port is *buying*, `price = buy_offer + (amount/capacity) * buy_offer / 2`; when *selling*, `price = sell_cost - (amount/capacity) * sell_cost / 2`. So a port pays more as its buying stock fills and charges less as its selling stock fills — simple, but it produces the right player incentive gradient. We extend this with BlackNova's parameterization (A.3). *(Reference description only: our normative pricing is §8, which flips the buy-side sign so that selling into a port lowers — never raises — what it pays. Implement §8, not this paragraph.)*

**Universe shape.** Hex-grid generation (`gen_hex_center(diameter)`) followed by `remove_warps(graph, warp_density, rnd)` to thin the lattice, using networkx. Coordinates map to sector IDs with sector 1 at origin. Ports placed by density roll; planets via `gauss(4.5, 1.5)` count in ~half the sectors. The hex-grid look is a deliberate aesthetic departure from TW2002's abstract graph; we instead follow the cluster-graph approach (A.5, A.6) to match TW2002's graph shape, building it on plain adjacency dicts (no networkx) to stay `mypy --strict`-clean.

**Client architecture.** Full-screen prompt-toolkit app at 15 FPS with a scene stack (title scene, terminal scene, battle scene), an `InstantCmd` single-keystroke command dispatcher, a TW2002 color theme module, and visual flourishes via `terminaltexteffects` (starfields, animated transitions). Notably it does *not* use Textual — confirming our framework choice gives us widgets (DataTable, Tree, Input, tabs, CSS layout, `textual serve` web hosting) that terminal-space had to hand-build.

**RPC layer.** Client and server share Pydantic models in `tspace/common/` and communicate via JSON-RPC (pjrpc) over aiohttp, with the server embeddable in-process for single-player ("local mode" just instantiates `Server` directly). This embedded-server pattern is exactly how we will do single-player while keeping multiplayer reachable.

### A.3 [BlackNova Traders](https://sourceforge.net/projects/blacknova/) ([cheevauva/blacknovatraders](https://github.com/cheevauva/blacknovatraders) mirror) — PHP/SQL web clone

A long-lived web clone whose economy constants were tuned by years of live games. From `config.php` and `port.php`:

**Parameterized linear pricing.** Each commodity has `base_price`, `delta`, and `limit`: ore (11, 5), organics (5, 2), goods (15, 7), energy (3, 1). Port price = `base ± delta * (port_stock / limit) * inventory_factor`, minus when the port sells (stock depresses price), plus when it buys. This is the same shape as terminal-space's formula but with independently tunable elasticity per commodity — we adopt this parameterization.

**Planet production system.** From `sched_planets.php`: colonists produce per tick as `min(colonists, colonist_limit) * colonist_production_rate (0.005)` allocated across ore/organics/goods/energy/fighters/torpedoes by player-set percentage sliders, with organics consumption (0.05/colonist-tick) and starvation death rate (0.01) when food runs out, colonist reproduction at 0.0005, and credit interest at 1.0005 per tick compounded. This gives us a complete, proven planetary economy spec.

**Equipment price list** (useful baselines): fighters 50, torpedoes 25, armor 5, colonists 5, beacons 100, Genesis torpedo 1,000,000, warp editor 100,000, mine deflector 10, escape pod 100,000, fuel scoop 100,000. (BNT prices everything, colonists included, as a buyable item; in our model colonists are **not** a commodity — the `5` is reread as the per-head **recruitment incentive** paid to enlist a willing colonist, §4.2, not a purchase price.)

**Combat.** `attack.php` resolves attacks with percentile rolls plus device checks (emergency warp device triggers on a failed roll and teleports the defender to a random sector) and 10–20% salvage of a destroyed ship's cargo. Simple, swingy, fun — a good Phase-3 baseline before TW2002's more elaborate odds tables.

### A.4 [Alien Assault Traders](https://github.com/tarnus/aatraders) (tarnus/aatraders) — PHP, BNT/NGS fork

A 75 MB fork of BlackNova with heavy feature accretion: admin tooling (port price re-seeding, universe editors, IP bans, player/planet reports), team systems, shoutboxes, 3D galaxy map imaging. The main lesson is curatorial: AAT shows what twenty years of feature creep on the BNT economy looks like. We mine it for admin/sysop feature ideas (the `admin/` directory is effectively a sysop-tooling catalog) but treat its gameplay code as redundant with BNT.

### A.5 [SectorWars](https://github.com/leonard4/SectorWars) (leonard4/SectorWars) — C++, early-stage

Rudimentary, but it preserves a valuable artifact: `TW Sector Algorithm.txt`, a community description of how TW2002-style universes are believed to be generated. Summarized: allocate ~1000 sectors; repeatedly pick groups of 5–25 unassigned sectors and randomly interlink within each group (bidirectionally); name each group as a region/nebula (40–200 groups); then connect each group to 1–5 other groups with random inter-group warps; finally run pathfinding from a fixed origin to verify full reachability. This cluster-then-bridge algorithm produces the authentic TW2002 feel — local neighborhoods, sparse bridges, natural chokepoints — and is the core of our big bang (Section 5).

### A.6 [ExchangeConflict2016](https://github.com/jzmiller1/ExchangeConflict2016) (jzmiller1) — Python, universe-gen experiments

A Python sandbox focused on generation. Its `bigbang.py` builds a networkx graph with explicit *deadends* (2-sector stubs), *rings* (cycles of size drawn from a weighted choice of {3,5,7,9}), and a connectivity pass that attaches every remaining sector to the connected component — a menu of graph motifs we reuse. It enforces sanity constraints (minimum 20 systems; deadends capped relative to universe size). It also demonstrates per-sector procedural star systems (via StarGen) and a `uniview.py` map debugger that renders the universe with networkx-viewer, coloring port sectors green and empty sectors red — we will build the same kind of dev-mode map inspector with Textual or matplotlib. Its `data/ships.json` shows config-driven ship definitions (holds min/max, shield max, hull cost), which we generalize.

### A.7 [drbeco/tradewars](https://github.com/drbeco/tradewars) — C clone + original 1986 TradeWars II BASIC source

The `tw2bas/` directory contains Chris Sherrick's original TW2 (RBBS QuixPlus lineage): `TW2.BAS`, `TW2SUB.BAS`, `TWVAR.BAS`, data files, and `TWINSTR.DOC`. The instructions document is primary-source material on the original rules: limited turns per day where each warp move or port landing costs one turn; sector fighters that must be fought (or retreated from at the cost of one fighter) before acting in a sector; trading in three commodities; buying fighters and cargo holds with profits; defending ports and sector chains for exclusive trading; the Cabal NPC menace dwelling in a region of space, with a 100-point bounty per Cabal fighter destroyed and 100,000 points for defeating them. The 500-sector universe (`S(500,1)` in TWVAR.BAS, TWMAP500 tooling) confirms the classic scale. The Cabal is the design ancestor of TW2002's Ferengi; our NPC faction follows the same role — a hostile NPC empire occupying defensible territory that strong players can raid.

### A.8 Cross-cutting conclusions

Three independent codebases (twclone, terminal-space, ExchangeConflict) converge on the same generation stack — seeded RNG, graph library, validation pass — and two converge on the same pricing shape (linear in stock ratio). The two most complete games (twclone, terminal-space) both separate a pure rules core from transport and UI, and both treat single-player as an embedded server. Where they differ (hex grid vs. cluster graph; order-book economy vs. simple regen), we choose the option closer to the TW2002 foundation for defaults and keep the alternative reachable via configuration.

## Appendix B — Lightspeed (MicroProse, 1990): What We Took and What We Left

Where Appendix A records what the TradeWars lineage gave us — the warp graph, the port economy, the turn engine — this appendix records the **second source** that shaped the design: *Lightspeed*, MicroProse's 1990 interstellar action-adventure (designed by Sandy Petersen and Andy Hollis). TW2002 supplies the *skeleton*; Lightspeed supplies much of the *flesh* of the exploration-first layer — the engine-room ship model (§4.1), the planetary taxonomy and orbital starbases (§4.2), the ship/starbase class schema (§4), and a good deal of the alien/encounter texture (§6, §10). This appendix is the audit trail: every Lightspeed-sourced idea, where it landed in this document, and — just as importantly — what we deliberately discarded or transformed.

### B.1 The source, and how it was read

*Lightspeed* is a first-person, real-time 3-D space game: the player flies a lone *Trailblazer*-class scout sent ahead of a *Conestoga* colony transport to prepare an alien star cluster for human settlement. The mission has two halves — **find a habitable world and gather the raw materials a colony needs** (by mining unclaimed planets and trading with aliens), and **make the cluster safe** (by signing peace treaties with friendly aliens and eliminating or weakening hostile ones). The ship is maintained not with money but by **replacing burned-out components**, obtained through alien trade or salvaged from destroyed enemies. (General reference: the game's MobyGames entry, <https://www.mobygames.com/game/1681/lightspeed/>.)

Our source text is the game's printed manual, rendered to Markdown from the 58-page archive.org scan and held locally at `lightspeed-analysis/manuals/Lightspeed_Manual.md`. **When citing it, link the public copy at <https://archive.org/details/lightspeed-1990-manual>**; section numbers below (e.g. *manual §2.4.5*) refer to that manual. A companion file, `lightspeed-analysis/Alien_Race_Reference.md` — a seventeen-species Hyades-cluster catalogue distilled from the manual's "A Few Aliens" chapter (manual §4) and related lore — was the stress-test roster for the species parameter set (§6.1); it is Lightspeed-derived and is referenced here as the practical proof that the §6 schema can express a real, idiosyncratic cast as pure data. A second companion file, `lightspeed-analysis/Lightspeed_Alien_Dialogue_Reference.md` — each species' branching conversation prose and verb menus extracted verbatim from the game's per-species `.bin` data files — was the stress-test corpus for the **dialogue system** (§6.7), and is the proof that standing-keyed, persona-voiced conversation can likewise be expressed as config rather than code.

The guiding instruction throughout was **inspiration, not imitation, and never a burden on the player**: take Lightspeed's systemic ideas, reimplement them cleanly against our turn-based / TUI / TW2002-derived constraints, and drop anything that only made sense in a real-time 3-D cockpit.

### B.3 What we deliberately left behind

Lightspeed is a real-time 3-D action game; we are a turn-based TUI. Several of its systems were explicitly **ruled out** during design (the engine-room session call-out was specific about this) because they only make sense at a flight stick:

- **The blaster-turret cockpit and its point-defense bay** (manual §2.2, §2.4.2) — we model **no point-defense**; enemy missiles are answered by screens and speed alone, keeping the weapon set to Main Gun + homing missiles + screens (§4.1).
- **Pilotable fighters and kamikazes** (manual §2.3, §3.1.3) — no launch-and-fly sub-craft. Sector fighters return only in their classic TW2002 deployable-stack role (§10, Phase 3), not as a piloted cockpit.
- **The cockpit/targeting layer** — radar scope, holographic viewer, crosshairs/reticule, target-change, manual aiming (manual §2.1, §2.2.2, §3.3.4). Combat is resolved as rounds against derived aspects, not by aiming in real time.
- **Real-time action combat itself**, the 3–13-second main-gun reload, "shoot likely-looking places to find vulnerabilities" (manual §3.3.4) — abstracted into round-based resolution with firing-arc *positioning* rather than twitch aiming.

### B.4 Where we transformed it (deliberate divergences)

Some ideas were kept but **reshaped** to fit our turn-based / TUI constraints and the TW2002-derived foundation. These are noted so a reader of the manual isn't surprised by the differences:

- **Fuel → turns.** Lightspeed meters travel by **fuel per jump** (manual §1.2.1, §2.4.5); we keep TW2002's **turns-per-warp** instead (movement costs turns, not fuel). The Spindrive therefore drives `turns_per_warp`, and "fuel economy" has no analog — the standing latinum sinks are components, ships, and repair (§8).
- **Four raw materials → the sacred trio.** Lightspeed's colony commodities are **Metal / Organics / Radioactives / Water** (manual §1.4.6). We hold the line at TW2002's **Fuel Ore / Organics / Equipment** and express Lightspeed's four as `planet_type` *yield-and-habitability shaping* (radioactive hot worlds read as Fuel-Ore-rich, metal-rich cold worlds as Equipment-rich, watery cool worlds as high-habitability), adding **no fourth commodity** (§4.2).
- **Components-as-barter → latinum economy.** Lightspeed trades components directly on a 1–4 Demand/Supply balance, with **computer disks** as the universal home-base trade good and **fuel always worth 1 component** (manual §1.4.2–§1.4.5, §2). We denominate everything in **gold-pressed latinum** with artifact/discovery barter on top (§8), and map a discovery's rarity tier to a component tier for barter equivalence — keeping component scarcity without the balance-marker mini-game.
- **Per-converter system coupling → one global bonus.** The manual wires Spindrive output into screens, thrusters, and the main gun *through each system's converters*, with separate per-component tables (manual §2.4.1, §2.4.3–§2.4.5). We collapse that web into a **single legible Spindrive-efficiency buff** applied across the board (§4.1), so the player reasons about "tune your engine and your whole ship improves" rather than tracking a converter network.
- **Single star cluster → warp-graph universe with bands.** Lightspeed's playfield is one navigable cluster; ours is the TW2002 cluster-and-bridge **warp graph** with **distance bands** (§5) driving rarity, danger, and ownership. Lightspeed's "encounters likelier near an alien's worlds" survives as territory-weighted encounter tables (§10).
- **Hand-authored branching scripts → conditional variant pools.** Lightspeed's dialogue is a fixed branching script baked into each species' `.bin` file, with only its `%s` slots variable. We keep its standing-keyed branching and per-species voice but reauthor it as a **`dialogue_pack`** of conditional line entries with **variant pools and a recency ring** (§6.7), so repeat encounters rephrase rather than replay, the whole corpus lives in a config dialogue file (`config/alien_dialogue_default.yaml`, merged onto the roster at load), and a `persona` fallback chain keeps authoring light — turning a static script into a data-driven, non-repeating conversation layer.
- **Starbase = a ship class, not a special structure.** Lightspeed starbases are immobile system defenders (manual §3.2.3); we generalize them into a `role=starbase` **`ship_class`** that reuses the engine-room model **minus thrusters/spindrive, plus a `fusion_reactor`** (§4.2) — and we make **"derelict" emergent** (missing/damaged components) rather than a stored type, so repair is ordinary engine-room work.

### B.5 The net

Lightspeed's lasting contribution to this design is a single, reusable mechanism — **the engine room of slotted subsystems built from a shared, fungible component vocabulary** — that simultaneously gives us localized combat damage, tangible repair, physical upgrades, a planetary-starbase model, and a deep-space salvage/barter economy (§4.1, §4.2). Around that core, its planetary taxonomy (§4.2) and its richly idiosyncratic alien cast — temperament, tactics, and **voiced, standing-keyed dialogue** alike (§6, §6.7) — gave the exploration-first layer its texture. Everything tied to its real-time cockpit was left at the door; everything systemic was reimplemented cleanly against our turn-based, TUI, TW2002-derived foundation.
